# ISO 13485 and working in a QMS {#ch16}

::: box-why
IEC 62304 tells you how to build medical device software, and ISO 14971 how to reason about its
risks. ISO 13485 is the system both of them run inside: the quality management system that
decides who may change a requirement, where the record of a review lives, what happens when a
test fails after merge, and what you say when an auditor asks how you know. It is easy to
think of it as the auditors' standard, and that would be a mistake: a software engineer meets the QMS
every day, in every pull request, every document edit and every problem report, long before
meeting an auditor. This chapter teaches the design controls of clause 7.3 from a software
engineer's side, document and change control, problem reports and CAPA, the difference between
verification and validation, and how to behave in an audit. MiniDAQ gets the same treatment it
got in the last two chapters, with one difference: this time the audit is real. You will audit
MiniDAQ against its own plan, and it will fail in four places.
:::

**A day in the life.** On a Tuesday morning the physics lead posts in the team channel: the new
crystals have better timing, so the default coincidence window should go from 4 ns to 3 ns. "It
is one number in a JSON file; can you just change it?" You can, in a minute. You open a change
request instead, and by lunch its impact analysis has found more than one number. The window
is a requirement's value, so the SRS changes. It is a risk control's parameter: a narrower window
loses true coincidences if the timing is worse than assumed, which is hazard H-07. Documents that
were written before the change and omit the window would silently mean 3 ns afterwards, so the
configuration schema needs a new minor version that keeps 4 ns for them. Several tests use the
default window and assert counts that will change, and the randoms measurement of Chapter 11 must
be repeated. The risk manager has to approve, because a risk control changes. The physics lead is
not pleased about the paperwork, until you show that the "one number" would have made every old
configuration file mean something new. The change goes in two days later, with its record, and
when the auditor samples it six months from now, the record answers every question before it is
asked. That is what a QMS is for.

## ISO 13485 and its design controls

### The intuition

A quality management system is the answer to the question "how does this company make sure that
what it ships is what it meant to ship, every time?" The answer cannot be "our engineers are
good", because good engineers leave, get tired and disagree. It has to be a set of written
procedures that say how the work is done, records that show it was done that way, and a loop that
finds and fixes the places where it was not. ISO 13485 is the standard for that system in medical
devices. It does not tell you how to design a scanner. It tells you what the system around the
design must do: plan the work, control the documents and the changes, check the results, and learn
from problems.

The idea that changes most for a software engineer is that the record is part of the product.
Code that works but whose review nobody can show is, to the quality system, code whose review did
not happen. That sounds bureaucratic until you are the engineer who must explain, three years
later, why a parameter has the value it has. Then the record is the only colleague who remembers.

### The structure of the standard

ISO 13485:2016 is the third edition. Its requirements are in clauses 4 to 8, in this book's words:

- **Clause 4, quality management system.** The system itself, its documentation (a quality
  manual, procedures, and a medical device file for each device type), the control of documents
  (4.2.4) and of records (4.2.5), and the validation of software used in the quality system
  (4.1.6).
- **Clause 5, management responsibility.** Top management's commitment, the quality policy and
  objectives, responsibilities and authorities, and the management review of the system.
- **Clause 6, resource management.** Competent people (and the records of their training), the
  infrastructure and the work environment.
- **Clause 7, product realization.** Planning, customer requirements, design and development
  (7.3), purchasing (7.4, which covers SOUP and suppliers), production and service, and the
  control of monitoring and measuring equipment.
- **Clause 8, measurement, analysis and improvement.** Feedback and complaints (8.2.1, 8.2.2),
  reporting to authorities, internal audit (8.2.4), monitoring of processes and product, control
  of nonconforming product (8.3), analysis of data, and corrective and preventive action (8.5.2,
  8.5.3).

Clause 7.1 asks for risk management throughout product realization, which is where ISO 14971
(Chapter 15) plugs in, and clause 7.3 is where IEC 62304 (Chapter 14) does: the software life
cycle is the design and development process of the software part of the device.

The standard has a regulatory purpose that the general quality standard ISO 9001 does not: its
requirements are what regulators accept as a quality system. The EU MDR requires a quality
management system of every manufacturer (Article 10(9)) and presumes conformity for the parts
that a harmonised standard covers; EN ISO 13485:2016 is harmonised (verify: with the amendment
listed in the current Official Journal entry). In the United States, the Quality Management
System Regulation (QMSR), the revised 21 CFR 820, incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference and
applies from 2 February 2026, replacing most of the old Quality System Regulation's own wording.
One consequence for your vocabulary: the QMSR uses the standard's terms, so the "design history
file" of the old regulation is now, formally, the design and development file of clause 7.3.10
(verify: the QMSR's definitions and FDA's guidance on the transition). Many teams, and this
book's plan, still say DHF; it means the same thing.

### Clause 7.3 for a software team

Clause 7.3 has ten subclauses. Each of them already has an artifact in MiniDAQ, because Chapters
1 to 15 built them; this chapter makes the mapping explicit.

- **7.3.2, planning.** A plan for the design and development: stages, reviews, verification,
  validation and transfer at each stage, responsibilities, and the methods to trace outputs to
  inputs. For software it is the software development plan of IEC 62304 (Chapter 14), and
  MiniDAQ's is `docs/regulatory/sdp.md`.
- **7.3.3, inputs.** Functional, performance, usability and safety requirements from the intended
  use, regulatory requirements, the outputs of risk management, and anything learned from similar
  designs. They must be complete, unambiguous, verifiable and not in conflict. MiniDAQ's inputs
  are the intended use, the regulatory context, the SRS and the performance budget.
- **7.3.4, outputs.** In a form that can be verified against the inputs, with the information for
  purchasing and production, acceptance criteria, and the characteristics that matter for safe
  use. For software: the architecture, the detailed design, the code, the data formats.
- **7.3.5, review.** Systematic reviews at planned stages, with representatives of the functions
  concerned and other specialists, and records of the results and the actions.
- **7.3.6, verification.** Evidence that the outputs meet the inputs, according to planned
  arrangements, with records of the methods, the results and the conclusions.
- **7.3.7, validation.** Evidence that the product meets the requirements of its intended use,
  done on representative product, before release, with clinical or performance evaluation as
  required.
- **7.3.8, transfer.** The outputs are verified as suitable for production before they become
  production specifications. For software: the release process, the build that produces what
  ships, and the installation instructions.
- **7.3.9, control of changes.** Changes are identified, their significance determined, and they
  are reviewed, verified, validated as needed and approved before they are implemented, with the
  effect on delivered product and on risk management evaluated.
- **7.3.10, the file.** A design and development file for each device type or family, which holds
  or references the records that show conformity and the records of the changes.

Notice how much of this a good software team does anyway. Requirements, design, reviews, tests
and releases are normal engineering. What the clause adds is that each is planned in advance,
done as planned, and recorded so that someone who was not there can check it.

::: box-mistakes
- **Reading 7.3 as a waterfall.** The clause asks for planned stages and records, not for one
  pass from inputs to outputs. An iterative process with reviews at each increment conforms, as
  long as the plan says so and the records show it. IEC 62304 makes the same point about its own
  activities.
- **Treating inputs as the product requirements document.** Design inputs include the outputs of
  risk management and the regulatory requirements. A requirement that comes from a risk control
  (SRS-CTL-030, the integrity verdict) is an input like any other, and must be traceable as one.
- **Writing the plan for an ideal team.** A plan that the team does not follow is worse than a
  modest plan that it does, because every deviation is a nonconformity. MiniDAQ made exactly this
  mistake, and the audit in this chapter finds it.
:::

## The design and development file

### What goes in it

The design and development file is less a document than an index. Clause 7.3.10 asks that it
contain or reference the records of design and development, so in practice it is a list: for each
part of the process, where the evidence is. Under the EU MDR the manufacturer also keeps
technical documentation, set out in Annexes II and III of the regulation, which the notified body
reviews; most of the design file's records end up referenced from it. Under the QMSR, the medical
device file of clause 4.2.3 and the design and development file of clause 7.3.10 replace the old
regulation's device master record and design history file.

For a software engineer, the useful question is: when someone asks how a requirement was met, can
you get from the question to the record in a few minutes? An index that answers this has to be
organized by what people ask, which is mostly by clause: where are the inputs, where is the
verification, where are the changes.

### A worked example: MiniDAQ's design file

MiniDAQ's design file is `docs/regulatory/qms/design-file.json`, from which
`tools/design_file.py --write` generates the readable `design-file.md`. It maps 18 clauses of ISO
13485 to evidence in the repository. Two entries show the idea:

| Clause | Evidence | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 7.3.6, verification | `docs/regulatory/trace-matrix.md`, `minidaq/tests/`, `fuzz/`, `ci/run_all.sh`, `.github/workflows/` | Every requirement traced to its tests or its measurement; six presets and static analysis on every change. |
| 7.3.7, validation | none yet | Validation is of the scanner in its use environment; MiniDAQ alone cannot be validated. |

The second row matters as much as the first. An index that leaves out validation looks complete
until an auditor asks for it; one that says "none yet" and why turns the gap into a plan.

An index rots like any other document, so the test `docs.design_file` checks it on every change.
It fails when an evidence path is not in the repository, when a design and development clause is
missing, when a clause has neither evidence nor a plan, and when a document under `docs/` is in
no clause at all. "In the repository" means tracked by git: an empty directory, or a file you
created but did not add, is not a record, because nobody else can see it. That rule was added
after the check passed on the author's machine with an empty `capa/` directory that git would
never have pushed.

### Document control

Clause 4.2.4 asks that documents are reviewed and approved before they are issued, that their
changes and current revision are identified, that the right version is available where it is used,
and that obsolete versions cannot be used by mistake. In a company the document management system
does this, with workflows and signatures. In a repository, git does most of it: the commit
identifies every change, the tag identifies what was released, and nobody can use an old version by
accident because the working tree has only one. What git does not do is say which version of the
*document* a reader is holding, or what changed between versions in words a reviewer can check.

therefore state a version at the top and end with a history
table, and `tools/design_file.py` checks both:

The rows must be numbered 1, 2, 3 without gaps, and the last row must be the version at the top.
That catches the two usual mistakes: a version raised without saying why, and a history row added
without raising the version. A third mistake needs history, not just the current text: a document
that changed but kept its version. CI catches it by comparing every controlled document with the
last release:


`ci/run_all.sh` and the `document-control` job of the GitHub workflow run this against the last
`book-v` tag. Several changes in one release may share a version, which is why the comparison is
against the release and not against the previous commit: the version identifies what a reader of a
release holds, and a reader never holds a commit between releases.

### Records

A record is evidence that something was done: a test log, a review's result, a signed approval, a
measurement. Clause 4.2.5 asks that records stay legible, identifiable and retrievable, that changes
to a record stay identifiable, that they are kept for at least the lifetime of the device as the
manufacturer defines it (and not less than two years from release), and that confidential health
information in them is protected. Three consequences reach your code and your habits:

- **A record is never corrected in place.** If a test log is wrong, you write a new record that
  says so; if a history row has a typo, the next version's row corrects it. A record that changes
  silently is no longer evidence of anything. Exercise 16.5 makes this a check.
- **Generated records are checked against their sources.** MiniDAQ's trace matrix and known
  anomalies list are generated, and `docs.trace` and `docs.anomalies` fail when they are out of
  date, so the record cannot drift from what it records.
- **Logs can become records.** Chapter 6 said that the acquisition's audit trail might become a
  record if the quality system or a customer treats it as one. Then it needs the same properties:
  integrity (MiniDAQ's audit trail is appended to and never rewritten, and each entry carries its
  UTC time), retention, and access control, and, in the United States, the electronic records rules
  of 21 CFR Part 11 may apply (verify: which records of the NeuroLF fall under Part 11, and how the
  QMSR treats them).

A raw measurement is a record too. That is why every measurement in this book keeps its raw output
under `measurements/`, next to the number the text quotes: the number is a claim, the output is the
record.

### Software used in the quality system

The scripts in `tools/` decide whether a release may go out: if `trace_matrix.py` misses an
untested requirement, a defect ships with a green build. Clause 4.1.6 asks that software used in the
quality system be validated for its intended use before first use and after changes, with an
approach proportionate to the risk of its use, and with records. MiniDAQ's checks are tested in two
ways: each one is run on the repository on every change, and each was shown to fail when its rule
is broken, which this book's milestones record ("`docs.risk` fails when you break what it checks").
The exercises of Part III reimplement several of them against fixtures, with tests. That is less
than a validation record: the checks have no requirements of their own and no test suite in
`tools/` (assumption: confirm with team how the NeuroLF validates its QMS tools, and whether they
count as software of the quality system or of the device).

::: box-mistakes
- **Treating the wiki as the document.** A page anyone can edit, with no version and no approval,
  is a note, not a controlled document. Notes are fine; requirements in them are not.
- **Fixing the record instead of writing a new one.** Editing a past test result, a closed problem
  report or an old history row destroys the evidence you are trying to improve.
- **Keeping evidence where only you can see it.** A screenshot on your laptop, an untracked file or
  an empty directory is not a record. If it is not in the system of record, it did not happen.
- **Versioning by date or by commit.** A reader needs to know which edition of a document they
  hold and what changed since the last one they read. A commit hash says neither.
:::

## Change control and impact analysis

### The intuition

A change to a design that works is the most dangerous thing a team does. The original design was
reviewed, verified and validated as a whole; a change is usually reviewed as a diff, and what the
diff does not show is everything that relied on the old behavior. Change control exists to make
someone ask, before the change, what else it touches, and to record the answer so that the next
person can check it.

Clause 7.3.9 asks that design changes are identified and their significance determined (for the
function, performance, usability, safety and regulatory requirements of the device and its intended
use), that they are reviewed, verified and, where needed, validated, and approved before they are
implemented. The review includes the effect on parts already made and product already delivered,
and on the inputs and outputs of risk management. IEC 62304 asks the same of software in its
clauses 6 and 8: a change request, an analysis of its effect on safety and on the software items,
and verification that repeats whatever the change may have broken.

### When a change needs a request

Not every edit needs a change request; a team that filed one per commit would stop committing.
MiniDAQ's procedure, `docs/regulatory/changes/README.md`, draws the line where the risk is:

- every change to a released version;
- every change to a requirement, a risk control, an item's safety class, a SOUP item, an interface
  or a data format, even before a release;
- code corrections that a problem report or a CAPA calls for.

Changes only to tests, tools and procedures that a CAPA calls for are recorded in the CAPA instead,
and editorial changes need a new document version, not a request. Everything else is reviewed in
the pull request, against the checklist at the end of the procedure.

### The impact analysis

The impact analysis is the part of a change request that does the work. MiniDAQ's template has a
section for each thing a change can touch, and the rule is that every section is filled in, with
"none" and the reason where nothing changes:

- **Requirements:** new, changed and removed requirements, and the ones whose verification must be
  repeated.
- **Software items and safety class:** the items that change, their class, and whether segregation
  is affected.
- **Risk:** the hazards and risk controls affected, and whether any estimate changes.
- **SOUP:** items added, removed or changed, with their known-anomaly review.
- **Interfaces and data formats:** the API, the configuration schema, the list-mode format, and
  whether old clients and old files still mean what they meant.
- **Documents:** every controlled document that needs a new version.
- **Regulatory:** whether the change could be significant for a notified body or for the FDA
  (Chapter 13, Exercise 13.2).

"None, because" is the most valuable answer in the template. "SOUP: none" tells a reviewer nothing;
"SOUP: none, the parser already accepts the new field" tells them what you checked.

### A worked example: CR-0003, the control API 1.1

The internal audit later in this chapter found that the control API's status had gained four
top-level fields between Chapters 9 and 15 (`detector_counters`, `sorter`, `sink_counters` and
`integrity`) while every response still said API 1.0. ADR 0003 says that new response fields come
with a new minor version. The defect is small and nothing failed, which is exactly why it went
through: no test compared the status with a list of what version 1.0 contains, because there was no
such list.

The change request is `docs/regulatory/changes/CR-0003-api-1.1-status-fields.md`. Its description
is one paragraph: the API becomes 1.1, the status has exactly the fields of a list in the code, each
marked as always present or present while its part exists, and a test compares real statuses with
the list. Its reason names the cost of not changing: the integrity verdict is a risk control, and a
client must be able to tell a server without it. Its impact analysis is the interesting part:

- **Requirements:** a new one, SRS-CTL-033, "the status has exactly the fields of its API
  version", and the protocol requirements SRS-CTL-020 to SRS-CTL-023 whose tests must be repeated.
- **Risk:** H-01's control RC-01 becomes part of the interface contract, which supports RC-05
  (software that reads the data checks the verdict first). No estimate changes.
- **Interfaces:** the major version is unchanged, so every 1.0 client keeps working; the content of
  the status does not change, only its version says what it contains.
- **Regulatory:** an interface version that documents what a released interface already carries is
  not significant (assumption: confirm with regulatory affairs).

The decision needs two people: the software lead, and the risk manager because a risk control is
affected. The code that came out of it is a list that a reviewer can read in ten seconds:

```{.cpp include="minidaq/include/minidaq/control/control_protocol.hpp" snippet="status_fields"}
```

and a test that makes the list the truth, so that the next field cannot be added silently:

```{.cpp include="minidaq/tests/unit/test_control_protocol.cpp" snippet="status_fields_test"}
```

The test is the corrective part of the change. Without it, CR-0003 would fix today's version number
and leave the cause in place.

### A worked example: CR-0002, a SOUP upgrade

The second change request, CR-0002, moves nlohmann/json from 3.11.3 to 3.12.0. It shows a different
kind of impact analysis: almost nothing in MiniDAQ changes, and almost everything must be verified
again. No requirement changes, but every requirement of the items that use the library
(configuration, service, protocol, output, audit and server) is verified again by the full test
suite. No code changes, but the known-anomaly review of Chapter 14 is repeated for the new release,
the SOUP list gets a new version, and the SBOM changes because it is generated from the pin.

Making the change found a defect in the build, not in MiniDAQ. CMake's FetchContent kept the
archive's file times, so after the pin changed, the build tool saw nothing newer and the link failed
with symbols from both versions of the library. A clean build of every preset fixed it, and the CI
chain now deletes its build directories first. That is a typical result of verifying a "trivial"
change properly: the change was right, and the process that builds it was not.

### Who approves

A design change is approved by the people the procedure names, before it is implemented. In
MiniDAQ's procedure, the software lead approves every change request; a change that affects a risk
control, a safety class or the intended use also needs the risk manager; one that may be
significant for a notified body or the FDA also needs regulatory affairs. In a company, a change
after release usually goes to a change control board, where quality, regulatory, engineering and
often service and manufacturing decide together, and the approvals are signed records (assumption:
confirm with team who approves which changes in your company). The author of a change does not approve
it. That holds even for one-person teams, and it is one of the places where MiniDAQ cannot conform,
as the audit will show.

### The status of a request

A change request is a record with a life: proposed, approved or rejected, implemented. MiniDAQ's
check, `tools/check_changes.py` (test `docs.changes`), enforces the parts of that life that a
machine can see:

```{.python include="tools/check_changes.py" snippet="check"}
```

Every requirement, hazard and risk control a request names must exist; an approved or implemented
request cannot have a pending decision; an implemented request must name its commits, which must be
in the repository, and the tests of its verification, which must exist. Because a commit cannot name
its own hash, a request is committed as `approved` with the implementation, and a follow-up commit
sets it to `implemented` with the hash. CR-0001, the integrity verdict of Chapter 15, was recorded
after the fact when change control arrived in this chapter, and says so in its first line. A record
written late is weaker than one written on time, but much stronger than one that pretends.

::: box-mistakes
- **Writing the analysis after the code.** An impact analysis written to describe a finished
  change finds what the change did, not what it touches. Do it first; update it if the work finds
  more.
- **"Only a default."** A default is a behavior of every document that omits the value. Changing it
  changes the meaning of every old configuration file, unless the schema's version says which
  default applies.
- **Empty sections.** A blank "Risk" section and a "Risk: none" section look the same to the author
  and different to an auditor. Write the reason.
- **Missing the verification to repeat.** The tests of the changed code are the easy part. The hard
  part is the tests of everything that relied on the old behavior, and the measurements that assume
  it.
- **Approving your own change.** The person who wrote the analysis is the one least likely to see
  what it missed.
:::

## Problem reports and CAPA

### Two processes, one loop

A problem report records one problem: what happened, where, how bad it is for safety, and how it was
resolved. IEC 62304's problem resolution process (clause 9, Chapter 14) asks for exactly this, for
every problem found in the software, before or after release. A CAPA, corrective and preventive
action, asks a different question: *why did the process let this through, and where else does the
same cause act?* Clause 8.5.2 of ISO 13485 asks that nonconformities are reviewed, their causes
determined, actions taken without undue delay in proportion to their effects, the actions verified
so that they do not harm the device's safety or performance, and their effectiveness reviewed.
Clause 8.5.3 asks the same for potential nonconformities: the preventive part.

Not every problem needs a CAPA. A typo in a log message needs a problem report and a fix. A CAPA is
for causes that will produce more problems: a pattern, a defect that reached the field, a process
that was not followed. The CAPA procedure, `docs/regulatory/capa/README.md`, says when to open one.

### A good problem report

A problem report is read by people who were not there, often years later. The good ones share four
habits:

- **The title states the defect, not the symptom.** "A sequence jump of 2^31 was counted as two
  billion lost packets" (MDQ-PR-002), not "Strange loss counts".
- **The description says how to see it again.** The input, the version, the condition. MDQ-PR-001
  names the input `6e688` and the exception that escaped.
- **The safety evaluation is a sentence with a reason.** "A crash during configure is a lost scan if
  it happens at the scanner; no wrong value was ever accepted." Safety relevance is a judgment, and
  the reason is what lets a reviewer check it.
- **The resolution names its verification.** A test by name, a measurement by file.

A problem report can be wrong, and then it is corrected with a record. MDQ-PR-005, about
nlohmann/json, was evaluated in Chapter 14 on a cause marked "verify". When this chapter read the
library's code, the cause turned out to be wrong; the report now says so, and the decision that
rested on it was checked again. The "verify" mark did its job: the claim was checked before anything
important depended on it.

### The parts of a CAPA

MiniDAQ's CAPA template has nine sections: the problem, the investigation (including where else
the cause acts), the root cause (why the process let it happen), the correction, the corrective
action, the preventive action, the verification that the actions are in place and broke nothing,
the effectiveness check (a criterion, a date and later the result), and the closure.

The distinction that people get wrong most is between correction and corrective action. Fixing the
two flaky tests is a correction. Changing the coding standard and adding a check so that no test can
assert after a fixed sleep is a corrective action. A CAPA whose corrective action is a correction
has fixed the symptom and recorded that it fixed the cause.

### A worked example: CAPA-0001, tests that depend on the speed of the machine

The internal audit found that two test failures after merge had been fixed without problem reports
(finding A-4): `Pipeline.DropPolicyCountsEveryLostWord` on a fast GitHub runner and Exercise 10.4's
`FastSorter.FastEnough` on the book's machine. Each was fixed at once, which felt efficient. The
audit asked the CAPA question: is the cause still acting?

The investigation did two things. It read every sleep in MiniDAQ's tests and in the exercises'
tests, and it ran the threaded tests under load with a new script, `tools/stress_tests.sh`: twice as
many busy loops as CPUs, sixteen tests at a time, each test repeated until it fails. At tag `ch15`,
the ThreadSanitizer build failed two of 48 tests in 100 repetitions
(`measurements/ch16/stress-before-tsan.txt`): one test slept 150 ms and then asserted that a latency
interval had been reported, and one asserted that a paced source had delivered 80% of the words due
in 0.2 s, which depends on how often the test's own thread gets the CPU. Reading found the same
pattern, a fixed sleep followed by an assertion that something happened, in five more tests that the
stress run did not catch.

The root cause is not "the developer wrote bad tests". It is that the coding standard said nothing
about time in tests and nothing checked it, so an assumption about speed held until a faster or
busier machine broke it, and each failure was fixed alone because no procedure asked whether the
cause acted elsewhere. That root cause points at actions:

- **Correction:** the seven tests wait for the condition they need, with a helper that turns a hang
  into a failure after a long deadline and otherwise returns as soon as the condition holds.
- **Corrective action:** rule 5.5 of the coding standard (a test never asserts after a fixed sleep,
  and every sleep in a test states why it is there), and the test `tests.waits`, which fails for a
  sleep without a reason.
- **Preventive action:** the stress run becomes part of the release verification in the SDP, so the
  next timing assumption fails on the book's machine before it fails in CI.


only a hang should reach the deadline. The check that keeps the rule is shorter still:

which is the point of the exercise. The CAPA records that residual risk instead of hiding it.

The effectiveness check has a criterion that can fail: the stress run passes at the implementing
commit, and no MiniDAQ test fails in CI for a timing reason before release 0.4.0. It is due at that
release, and until then the CAPA is `implemented`, not `closed`. Closing it today would record that
the cause is gone when nobody has yet seen whether it is.

### Root causes that are not

`tools/check_capa.py` (test `docs.capa`) checks a CAPA the way a quality engineer reads one:


Most of its rules are about fields and sections, and about the problem reports and the CAPA naming
each other. Two are about the thinking. A root cause that blames a person ("human error",
"carelessness") is rejected, because a person's mistake is a symptom: the question is why the
process let the mistake through. And the corrective and preventive actions must name a file of the
repository that they change, because an action that changes nothing a reviewer can see, such as "be
more careful" or "training", changes nothing. Training can be part of an action; it is never all of
it.

::: box-mistakes
- **Fixing without recording.** The fix is quick and the report feels like overhead, but a problem
  without a record cannot be trended, and a pattern of three becomes visible only when all three are
  written down.
- **"Human error" as the root cause.** Ask "why" once more: why could the error be made, and why did
  nothing catch it?
- **A correction labeled as corrective action.** If the action only fixes the instances found, the
  cause is still there.
- **An effectiveness check that cannot fail.** "No new problems so far" is also true if nothing
  changed. Name a number or a check, and a date.
- **Closing on implementation.** The actions being done is verification. Effectiveness is shown
  later, by evidence that the problem did not come back.
:::

## Verification and validation

### The intuition

Verification asks *did we build the thing right?* Validation asks *did we build the right thing?*
More precisely: verification shows that the design outputs meet the design inputs, and validation
shows that the device meets the needs of its users in its intended use. A sorter can meet every
requirement in the SRS (verified) and still be the wrong sorter if the requirements missed that the
clinic scans children, whose smaller bodies change the count rates and the scatter (not validated).

The two are different in what they compare, who does them and on what:

| | Verification (7.3.6) | Validation (7.3.7) |
|---|---|---|
| Compares | Outputs with inputs | The device with user needs and intended use |
| Question | Does the design do what the requirements say? | Does the device do what the clinic needs? |
| Typical evidence | Tests, analyses, inspections, reviews | Clinical or performance evaluation, usability validation, system tests in the use environment |
| Done on | Any level: unit, item, system | Representative product: production units or equivalents, in real or simulated use |
| Can fail when | The code is wrong | The requirements are wrong |

Software engineers do most of the verification. Validation is done on the scanner, with phantoms and
clinical images and the people who will use it, and software engineers support it with tools,
fixes and explanations. IEC 62304 stops at software system testing, which is verification;
validation of the device is outside its scope.

### A worked example: what MiniDAQ can and cannot show

MiniDAQ's verification is its trace matrix: each of its 122 requirements traced to the tests or the
measurement that verify it, with every test run in six configurations on every change. That is
strong verification evidence. Its validation evidence is nothing, and the design file says so: a
data acquisition component cannot be validated alone, because its intended use is to be part of a
scanner that produces images a physician reads. Validation of the NeuroLF includes MiniDAQ's
equivalent as one part of the system, tested with phantoms: Part IV builds the system tests, and
Chapter 30 measures the image quality that validation would rest on).

The trap in between is a test that looks like validation and is not.
`CoincidenceSorter.MatchesTheSimulatorsGroundTruth` compares the sorter's output with the true
coincidences of a simulation. It verifies SRS-SRT-010: the sorter does what its requirement says,
for inputs the simulator produces. It does not show that the requirement is right for the scanner,
because the simulator was written from the same assumptions as the requirement. A validation would
compare with physics the team did not write: a measured phantom.

::: box-mistakes
- **Calling system tests validation.** Tests against requirements are verification, however
  realistic their data. Validation is against user needs, on representative product.
- **Validating with the design's own assumptions.** A simulator written by the team verifies the
  code against the team's model; it cannot show that the model is right.
- **Validating once.** A design change can invalidate an earlier validation. The change request's
  impact analysis must say whether validation is repeated, and why not when it is not.
:::

## Audits

### Three kinds of audit

An audit is a systematic check of whether the work follows its requirements, done by someone who
did not do the work. Three kinds reach a software team:

- **Internal audits** (clause 8.2.4): the company audits itself, at planned intervals, against its
  own procedures and the standard. The auditors must not audit their own work.
- **Notified body audits** under the EU MDR: certification and surveillance audits of the quality
  system, reviews of the technical documentation, and unannounced audits (verify: the frequency set
  by Annex IX of the MDR).
- **Regulatory inspections**: FDA inspections in the United States, and audits under the Medical
  Device Single Audit Program (MDSAP), in which one audit serves several regulators (verify: which
  apply to the NeuroLF).

Auditors sample. Nobody can check everything, so an auditor picks a thread (a requirement, a change,
a complaint) and follows it through the records until it ends or breaks. A software engineer is
usually called in when the thread reaches the code: "show me how this requirement was verified",
"who reviewed this change", "how do you know the released binary is the one you tested".

### How to behave

Most advice about audits fits in a few rules, and all of them follow from one idea: the auditor
checks the system, not you, and the system's records speak better than you do.

- **Answer the question that was asked.** Short, true, and then stop. A long answer opens threads
  the auditor did not ask about, and a nervous one sounds like hiding.
- **Show the record.** "The test is `CoincidenceSorter.MatchesTheSimulatorsGroundTruth`, here is its
  result in the release record" beats any explanation. If you cannot show it, say so.
- **Never guess.** "I don't know; I will find out" or "my colleague owns that, I will get them" is a
  good answer. A wrong guess is a finding; worse, it is a finding about trust.
- **No hedges.** "It should be tested", "probably", "I think" tell an auditor that you have not
  checked. Check before you answer, or say that you will.
- **Report bad news yourself.** If you find a nonconformity while preparing, tell your quality team
  before the audit, and in the audit say what was found and what is being done. A problem that the
  company found and is fixing is a sign that the system works; the same problem found by the auditor
  is a sign that it does not.
- **Do not argue the finding in the room.** If you disagree, say so calmly, show the evidence, and
  let the quality team respond through the formal process.

### A worked example: MiniDAQ's internal audit

This chapter audited MiniDAQ against its own plan, from Chapter 1 to tag `ch15`. The report is
`docs/regulatory/qms/internal-audit-2026-10-25.md`, and its method was sampling: the sorter from
requirement to test, every controlled document against its git history, every commit that fixed a
test, the control API against its versioning decision, and the SOUP review against the library's
source. The auditor was the book's author, which clause 8.2.4 does not allow, and the report says so
first: in a teaching project nobody else can audit, so the findings are weaker evidence than an
independent audit's.

It found four nonconformities and two observations:

- **A-1:** the SDP required a pull request reviewed by someone other than its author for every
  change, and every change went to the main branch directly. Nothing was built wrong; the plan
  described a team that MiniDAQ does not have.
- **A-2:** seven of the ten documents that were controlled at the audit (tag `ch15`; this chapter
  adds three more) had no change history, all of them written before Chapter 14; the performance
  budget had changed in four chapters without a version, and the MDQ-L1 specification had none.
- **A-3:** the API status gained fields without a new version (now CR-0003).
- **A-4:** two flaky-test fixes had no problem reports (now CAPA-0001).
- **O-1:** a problem report's evaluation rested on a claim marked "verify" that turned out to be
  wrong; the mark worked as intended.
- **O-2:** no validation evidence, as expected for a component.

Look at what the findings have in common. The design outputs were verified and traceable; every
finding is about how changes were recorded, not about what was built. That is typical of real audits
of good engineering teams, and it is why engineers who think "our code is fine" are surprised by
audits. The code was fine. The evidence that the process worked was not there.

Two of the findings needed CAPAs. CAPA-0002 takes A-1 and A-2 together, because they share a root
cause: the SDP of Chapter 14 described the process of a team before anyone compared it with how the
project worked, and nothing checked the document rules. Its corrective action is the honest one: SDP
version 3 states the review MiniDAQ actually does (the author's review of each change against the
change control checklist, recorded in the commit, and a second person's review at the end of each
Part), and says plainly that this is weaker than a team's review and accepted only for a teaching
project. Every commit since carries the line "Review: change control checklist, items 1 to 6." That
does not make MiniDAQ conform to what a team must do; it makes the plan true, so that the next audit
measures the practice against a plan that describes it.

::: box-mistakes
- **Volunteering.** Answering the next three questions before they are asked turns one sample into
  four.
- **Reconstructing records for the audit.** A record written the night before, and dated as if it
  were older, is falsification. A late record dated honestly is a finding; a backdated one is a much
  worse one.
- **Defending the code instead of the process.** The auditor rarely doubts that the code works. They
  ask how you know, and the answer is a record.
- **Treating the internal audit as a rehearsal.** Its findings are the cheapest ones the company
  will ever get. An internal audit that finds nothing has usually not looked.
:::

## Working in a QMS, day to day

Most of what this chapter describes reaches you as habits, not as clauses. A few that experienced
engineers in regulated teams share:

- **Name the record in the commit.** "Implements CR-0003", "fixes MDQ-PR-012", "CAPA-0001
  correction". A reviewer and an auditor can follow a commit to its reason only if the commit says
  it.
- **Write the problem report when you find the problem,** not when you fix it. The time between the
  two is when the report is most useful to others.
- **Ask when a process does not fit.** A procedure that cannot be followed should be changed through
  the system, not quietly ignored. That is how A-1 should have been handled in Chapter 14.

::: box-regulated
What the regulated setting adds to this chapter's examples:

- In a company, change requests, CAPAs and audit findings live in an electronic quality system with
  workflows, signatures and access control, not in Markdown files in the repository. MiniDAQ's files
  show the content and the checks; the tool and its validation are the company's (assumption:
  confirm with team which tool the NeuroLF uses and how it links to the code repository).
- Approvals are signed records, and the people who may sign are named in the quality system with
  their training records (clause 6.2). A merge button is not a signature unless the quality system
  says it is.
- A design change after release also needs a regulatory assessment: whether it is significant for
  the CE certificate or needs a new FDA submission (Chapter 13). The change request's "Regulatory"
  section feeds that assessment; it does not replace it.
- Complaints (8.2.2) and post-market surveillance (Chapter 13) feed problem reports and CAPAs from
  the field. Chapter 15's loop, from a field event back to the risk file, runs through these
  processes.
:::

::: box-askteam
1. Where do change requests, problem reports and CAPAs live, and how do they link to commits and

2. Who approves a design change to the DAQ software before release, and who after release? Is there

3. Which records does the software team own, and how long are they kept? Is the acquisition audit

4. How are the tools that generate or check design records (trace matrix, risk file checks)

## Summary {.unnumbered}

- ISO 13485:2016 is the quality management system of medical device manufacturers, accepted by the
  EU MDR and, since 2 February 2026, incorporated into the FDA's QMSR; clause 7.3 is design and
  development, and IEC 62304 is how software does it.
- The design and development file is an index from each clause to its evidence; MiniDAQ's maps 18
  clauses, says "none yet" for validation, and is checked on every change.
- Controlled documents carry a version and a history, and CI rejects a document changed since the
  last release without a new version; records are never corrected in place.
- A change request's impact analysis covers requirements, items and class, risk, SOUP, interfaces,
  documents and regulatory significance, with a reason for every "none", and is done before the
  code.
- Design changes are approved before implementation by the people the procedure names, never by
  their author alone; changes to risk controls also need the risk manager.
- A problem report records one problem with a safety evaluation; a CAPA finds why the process let it
  through, corrects, acts on the cause, and checks effectiveness with a criterion that can fail.
- Verification shows the outputs meet the inputs; validation shows the device meets its intended
  use, on representative product. MiniDAQ is verified and, as a component, not validated.
- In an audit, answer what is asked, show the record, never guess, and report bad news first.
- MiniDAQ's own audit found four nonconformities, all in how changes were recorded, and the chapter
  acted on each: a true SDP, versioned documents, API 1.1, and a CAPA that removed timing
  assumptions from the tests.
