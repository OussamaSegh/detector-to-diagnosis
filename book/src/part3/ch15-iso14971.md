# ISO 14971: risk management for software {#ch15}

::: box-why
ISO 14971 comes with IEC 62304, and the two cannot be separated: the
software safety class of Chapter 14 comes out of the risk analysis, and IEC 62304's clause 7
asks the software team to feed that analysis and to implement its controls. For a DAQ engineer
the standard is less abstract than it sounds. Every design choice you made in Part II can
become a hazard: a drop policy can lose data, a timing window can bias the counts, a clock
wrap can put events in the wrong place, and a writer can leave a damaged file that looks
complete. Wrong counts can mean a wrong diagnosis. This chapter teaches you to trace such a
choice from the code to the patient, to rate it when nobody can tell you how often software
fails, to design controls in software and prove that they work, and to keep all of it in a
risk file that the build checks. By the end, MiniDAQ has nine analyzed hazards, twenty risk
controls linked to requirements and tests, an FMEA of the sorter that found a real gap, and a
fault tree that found a single point of failure.
:::

**A day in the life.** A clinical site reports that the last frame of a dynamic brain scan looks
noisier than the others. The application specialist suspects the reconstruction, and the
reconstruction team suspects the data. You open the run record that MiniDAQ stored next to the
list-mode files. Its integrity verdict says `"complete": false`, with one loss counter:
`/detector_counters/packets_lost`. The audit trail has the same fact, recorded when the run
ended: the link lost packets during the last minutes of the scan, while a service laptop on the
same switch copied logs. MiniDAQ did what its risk controls say: it counted the loss, judged
the run incomplete and said so in two places. The reconstruction software never looked. You
write a problem report, and in the risk file you change one line: the probability of harm for
hazard H-01 was estimated on the assumption that every consumer checks the verdict, and that
assumption has just been shown to be weak. The control that relies on information for safety
needs a protective one beside it. That loop, from an event in the field back to an estimate in
the risk file, is what ISO 14971 asks for, and it is only possible because the verdict existed.

## What ISO 14971 is

### The intuition

A risk is the combination of how bad a harm is and how likely it is. Risk management is the
discipline of finding the risks a device brings, deciding which are acceptable, reducing the
others, and checking, for as long as the device is in use, that the decisions still hold. The
standard does not tell you which risks are acceptable. It tells you to decide in advance, write
the criteria down, apply them the same way to every risk, and keep a record that shows you did.
The record is the risk management file, and an auditor reads it the way you read a test log.

For software engineers the idea that changes most is that risk is about harm to people, not
about defects. A crash of the monitoring thread is a defect; it is a risk only through what
happens next: does the run continue without anyone seeing the counters, does the operator
repeat the scan, does incomplete data reach a physician? The analysis follows the defect out of
the code until it reaches a person or stops being able to.

### The process

ISO 14971:2019 is the third edition, and it describes one process for the whole life of a
device. Its clauses, in this book's words:

- **Clause 4, general requirements.** A risk management process, management that provides the
  resources and sets the policy for acceptable risk, competent people, a risk management plan
  for each device, and the risk management file.
- **Clause 5, risk analysis.** The intended use and the reasonably foreseeable misuse, the
  characteristics that affect safety, the hazards and hazardous situations, and an estimate of
  the risk of each.
- **Clause 6, risk evaluation.** Each risk against the criteria of the plan.
- **Clause 7, risk control.** Choosing controls, implementing and verifying them, evaluating the
  residual risk, a benefit-risk analysis where a risk stays unacceptable, the risks that the
  controls themselves introduce, and a check that every hazardous situation was considered.
- **Clause 8, overall residual risk.** All residual risks together, against the criteria and
  the benefits.
- **Clause 9, risk management review.** Before release: the plan was carried out, the overall
  residual risk is acceptable, and the methods to collect information after release exist. The
  result is the risk management report.
- **Clause 10, production and post-production.** Collecting information from production and
  use, and acting on it.

The 2019 edition moved most of its guidance into a separate document, ISO/TR 24971:2020, which
explains how to set up criteria, which techniques to use for hazard identification, and how to
handle information after release. The technical report is guidance, not requirements; you will
find it on the shelf of every risk manager. In the EU, EN ISO 14971:2019 with its 2021 amendment
adds annexes that relate the standard to the MDR's general safety and performance requirements
(Chapter 13), among them the MDR's demand to reduce risks "as far as possible", which leaves
less room for an acceptable-because-small argument than some older practice did (verify: the
Z annexes of your organization's copy).

### Where the software fits

The risk file belongs to the device, and the scanner's risk manager owns it. The software team
contributes in three ways, and IEC 62304's clause 7 names each of them:

1. **Analysis.** Which software items can contribute to a hazardous situation, and through which
   causes: wrong or missing function, failures of SOUP, failures of hardware that the software
   must detect, and misuse that the software makes possible.
2. **Control.** Risk control measures implemented in software, each one a requirement, each
   requirement verified.
3. **Change.** Every change to the software is analyzed for its effect on existing controls and
   for new causes of hazardous situations.

IEC/TR 80002-1:2009 is the guidance that applies ISO 14971 to software; it is old and partly
overtaken by the 2019 edition, but its examples of software causes are still useful (verify: a
revision is in preparation). For MiniDAQ, the software's part of the risk file is in
`docs/regulatory/risk/`, and its plan, `risk-management-plan.md`, states that in a real product
it would be one part of the scanner's file (assumption: confirm with team how your scanner's file
is organized).

## From hazard to harm

### The intuition

Four terms carry the analysis. The standard defines three of them, and its annex on risk
concepts uses the fourth; in this book's words:

- A **hazard** is a potential source of harm: data that is lost without notice.
- A **sequence of events** is what has to happen for the hazard to matter: packets are dropped
  on the link and the run ends normally.
- A **hazardous situation** is the circumstance in which someone is exposed to the hazard:
  images are reconstructed from incomplete data that is treated as complete.
- A **harm** is the injury or damage that can follow: a quantitation error contributes to a
  delayed or wrong diagnosis.

Software is never the harm, and it is rarely the hazard by itself. It is almost always a step
in the sequence of events. That is why a software hazard analysis that lists defects ("the
sorter may have a bug") says nothing: it has not followed the sequence to a situation and a
harm, so nobody can rate it or control it.

P1 is the probability that the sequence leads to the hazardous situation, P2 the probability that the situation leads to harm." width="60%"}
```

### Hazards in imaging software

For an acquisition system, the hazards fall into a small number of kinds, and it helps to walk
through each when you look for hazards in a design:

- **Silent data loss.** Counts lost on the link, in a queue, in the sorter or at the disk,
  without a counter or with a counter nobody reads.
- **Wrong timestamps.** Unwrapping errors, disorder between boards, clock offsets: they form
  the wrong coincidences and put events in the wrong place along the line of response.
- **Corrupted files.** Damaged blocks, truncated files under their final name, two runs written
  into one file set.
- **Mislabeled data.** Data attributed to the wrong patient, the wrong protocol, or the wrong
  kind of acquisition (a simulation stored as a scan).
- **Quantitation errors.** Biased windows, wrong corrections, settings other than those that
  reconstruction assumes: every count is there, and every count is slightly wrong.
- **Loss of control.** A scan stopped or reconfigured by a crash, a misbehaving client or an
  unauthorized party, so that the patient is scanned again.

MiniDAQ handles patient identity by not having it: list-mode files carry a run name, and the
link between a run and a patient is held by the scanner's workflow software (intended-use
statement, section Data). That is a risk control in its own right, inherent safety by design,
because software that has no patient identity cannot mislabel one. The hazards of
mislabeling that remain for MiniDAQ are the ones that put the wrong data behind a correct
name: two runs mixed in one file set (H-09) and simulated data stored as a scan (H-06).

### A worked example: MiniDAQ's hazards

The analysis starts from the preliminary hazards of the software classification in Chapter 14,

| H-02 | Wrong event times | HZ-1 | S2: P4, then P2 | closed |
| H-03 | Stored data corrupted | HZ-1 | S2: P4, then P1 | closed |

phantom catches systematic errors, and a repeated scan is possible (Chapter 14). Those are
controls outside the software, and the severities are teaching choices (assumption: confirm
with team). If the clinical expert decides that a quantitation error in a dementia work-up can
lead to a wrong treatment decision, H-01 becomes S3, the software class becomes C, and much
of Chapter 14 changes. That is the single judgment in this file that would move everything else,
which is why the plan gives it to a named role.

::: box-mistakes
- **Listing defects instead of hazards.** "Buffer overflow in the sorter" is a cause. Follow it
  to a hazardous situation and a harm, or it cannot be rated.
- **Stopping at the software boundary.** The harm happens to a patient, often hours later and
  in another system. The chain is incomplete until it reaches a person.
- **One hazard per component.** Hazards come from what the device does, not from how the code is
  divided. The sorter appears in H-01, H-02 and H-07.
- **Forgetting the controls outside the software.** Image review and quality control are why
  MiniDAQ's severities are S2. Write them down; they are assumptions somebody can check.
- **No misuse.** A misspelled configuration key (H-04) and a service laptop on the scanner's
  switch are foreseeable. The standard asks for reasonably foreseeable misuse, not only for use
  as intended.
:::

## Estimating risk when software failure cannot be estimated

### The intuition

A pump's motor fails at a rate you can measure on a test bench. Software does not wear out: a
defect is either in the code or not, and if it is there, it acts every time its conditions
occur. Nobody can give you a credible probability that the sorter has an off-by-one error. So
software risk estimation takes the software's failure as given, and asks how likely harm is
*after* it.

The standard's annex on risk concepts splits the probability of harm into two factors. P1 is the
probability that the sequence of events leads to the hazardous situation; P2 is the probability
that the situation leads to harm. For a software cause, the failure itself is taken as certain,
and what remains of P1 is whether its conditions occur and whether anything detects the failure
before the situation arises; P2 is about what happens in the clinic: does the reader notice the
artifact, does quality control catch the bias. This is the reasoning behind the common rule
that the probability of a software failure is 1. The 2006 edition of IEC 62304 stated it for the
classification; Amendment 1 replaced it with the consideration of risk controls outside the
software, but the idea stayed the common practice (verify: the wording in your copy of IEC 62304
and IEC/TR 80002-1).

### Criteria first

MiniDAQ's plan states its criteria before any hazard is rated, and stores them in the risk file
so that the checker applies exactly what the plan says:

- Severity S1 (negligible) to S4 (critical: death or life-threatening injury); probability of
  harm P1 (improbable) to P5 (frequent), per site and year of use.
- A risk is acceptable if the severity index plus the probability index is at most 5, and every
  risk is reduced as far as possible, acceptable or not.
- The probability that the software fails is taken as 1.

### A worked example: rating H-01

Before any control, how likely is harm from silently lost data? The software's part is taken as
certain: if packets are lost, MiniDAQ 0.2 counts them in a detector counter and does nothing else
with them. Packet loss on a busy link is expected (Chapter 9 lost 7,090 of 12,291 packets with
a 64 KiB receive buffer), so the sequence of events happens: P1 is high. Whether harm follows
depends on the reader: a loss of a few percent is hard to see in an image. The plan's scale makes this "probable", P4. S2 plus P4 is 6, so the
risk is not acceptable, and the software must do something.

After the controls of the next section, the loss still happens, but it is reported in the run
record and in the audit trail. Harm now needs a consumer that ignores a verdict written next to
the data. That is "remote", P2: S2 plus P2 is 4, acceptable. Notice what changed. The controls
did not make the link more reliable; they changed P1 by making the failure visible before the
hazardous situation. That is the pattern of most software risk controls: they rarely prevent a
failure, and they very often detect it.

## Risk control

### The order of preference

ISO 14971 asks you to consider controls in a fixed order:

1. **Inherent safety by design.** Remove the hazard or the cause: the sink that waits for a free
   buffer instead of dropping data (RC-04), the writer that never gives a file its final name
   before it is durable (RC-09), the absence of patient identity in MiniDAQ.
2. **Protective measures in the device.** Detect and respond: counters, the integrity verdict
   (RC-01), the fault on out-of-order singles (RC-07), CRCs on every block (RC-08).
3. **Information for safety.** Tell the user: the intended-use statement's instruction to check
   the verdict (RC-05), the "simulated" flag in every file's metadata (RC-15).

The order matters because each step relies more on people than the one before. A protective
measure works whether anyone reads the manual; information works only if someone reads it and
acts on it. Teams that reach for a warning in the user manual first are doing the order
backwards, and an auditor will ask what design change was considered.

outside the energy window, which are not a loss. Nothing required the decoder to count invalid
words, and the first draft of the integrity check did not look at them. The check now counts the decode stage's
filtered words as a loss, and a new requirement, SRS-FMT-013, holds the counter it relies on.

### Implementation and effectiveness

A risk control is verified twice. **Verification of implementation** shows that the control is
in the software: the requirement that implements it is tested, as every requirement is, and the
trace matrix shows it. **Verification of effectiveness** shows that the control does what the risk
estimate assumed: that the hazardous situation is actually detected or prevented. For RC-01 the
first is `RunIntegrity.EveryLossCounterMakesTheRunIncomplete`, which checks the function. The
second has to start from the hazard: a real loss on the link, a run that ends normally, and the
question of whether the verdict and the audit trail say so.

```{.cpp include="minidaq/tests/unit/test_acquisition_service.cpp" snippet="effectiveness"}
```

The difference is not pedantry. A unit test of `assess_integrity` would pass even if the service
never called it, or called it before the sink's counters were final. The effectiveness test runs
the whole path from a detector port to the audit record, and it would fail in either case.

Some controls cannot be shown effective by a test. RC-05 relies on people reading a document;
its evidence is an inspection of the intended-use statement, and the day in the life at the
start of this chapter is what happens when the inspection is the only evidence. RC-18, the audit
trail of control actions, detects misuse after the fact but prevents nothing, so the risk file
gives its effectiveness as an analysis and does not lower H-08's probability for it.

### Risks from risk controls

A control can create a risk of its own, and ISO 14971 asks you to look. RC-07 ends a run in a
fault when singles arrive out of time order, because the sorter cannot put them back. That
protects against wrong coincidences (H-02) and causes an interrupted scan (H-05). The risk file
accepts the trade, because a repeated scan is a known, visible harm and wrong coincidences are an
invisible one, but it records the trade in both hazards. The integrity verdict has the same
shape on a smaller scale: a verdict that is too strict, flagging runs that lose one packet in a
billion, teaches consumers to ignore it, which undoes RC-05. Whether a run with a tiny loss is
still usable is a clinical question for reconstruction; MiniDAQ reports the counters and does not
decide.

::: box-mistakes
- **A warning instead of a design change.** Information for safety comes last. Ask first
  whether the hazard can be removed, then whether it can be detected.
- **Counting is not controlling.** A counter that nobody reads is not a risk control. The
  control is the counter together with what reads it: the verdict, the alarm, the fault.
- **Testing the function, not the effect.** A unit test of the control verifies its
  implementation. Effectiveness needs a test that starts from the hazard.
- **Ignoring what the control breaks.** A new fault or a new verdict changes other hazards'
  sequences. Look for them, and write them down in both places.
- **Lowering a probability for a detective control that acts too late.** An audit trail that
  shows misuse the next day reduces nothing for the patient who was scanned.
:::

## FMEA and fault trees

### The intuition

A hazard analysis works top-down: from what can harm a patient to what in the software can
contribute. That finds the hazards but can miss causes, because it starts from the hazards you
thought of. Two classic techniques close the gap from either side. A **failure modes and effects
analysis** (FMEA, IEC 60812) works bottom-up: take each function of a component, ask how it can
fail, and follow each failure up to its effect. A **fault tree analysis** (FTA, IEC 61025) works
top-down from one undesired event, decomposes it through AND and OR gates into basic events, and
finds the combinations that are sufficient to cause it. Neither replaces the hazard analysis;
both feed it, with causes it missed and with combinations it did not see.

### A worked example: an FMEA of the sorter

The sorter's header lists its order of operations: energy gate, order check, windowing,
resolution of multiples, geometry, delayed window. Add the functions around it (closing the
windows at the end of a run, the accounting of its counters, the validation of its
configuration) and you have the rows of the FMEA. For each function, the analysis asks what
happens one picosecond or one keV either side of every comparison, what happens with zero, one,
two or many singles in every loop, and what decides every choice between candidates. Each row
records the failure mode, its effect and the hazard it can lead to, its cause, the severity, how
it would be detected, and the requirements that control it:

| Function | Failure mode | Effect | Detection | Controls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| windowing | partner exactly one window later missed | prompts and randoms biased (H-07) | unit test at the edge | SRS-SRT-001 |
| order check | out-of-order singles dropped without a count | silent loss (H-01) | accounting identity | SRS-SRT-009 |
| end of run | open windows never closed | last coincidences missing (H-01) | stop drains all data | SRS-PIPE-012 |
| multiples | a tie between pairs of equal energy broken another way | results not reproducible (H-07) | none before this chapter | SRS-SRT-012 (new) |

The last row is the reason to do an FMEA against the code rather than against memory. Reading
the resolution of multiples, you find this loop:

```cpp
} else if (const float sum = buf_[i].energy.count() + buf_[j].energy.count(); sum > best) {
  best = sum;  // ties keep the earlier pair
```

The comment states a rule, and the strict `>` implements it, but no requirement asked for it and
no test checked it. An optimization that walked the pairs in another order would have changed
the coincidences of every scan with tied multiples, and every test would still have passed. The
FMEA turned the rule into SRS-SRT-012 and a test, `CoincidenceSorter.TiesKeepTheEarlierPair`.

The FMEA has no occurrence column. Hardware FMEAs multiply severity, occurrence and detection
into a risk priority number, and IEC 60812:2018 itself warns against relying on that number alone
(verify). For software the occurrence of a defect is not a frequency, for the reasons of the
previous section, so MiniDAQ's FMEA rates severity and detection and asks of every row whether
an existing requirement controls it. Thirteen rows, twelve controlled by existing requirements,
one gap: that is a normal result for code that has been tested well.

### A worked example: a fault tree of silent data loss

The top event is the hazardous situation of H-01: incomplete data reconstructed as complete. It
needs two things together, an AND gate: data lost or altered on the way to the disk (gate G1),
and the loss not noticed before reconstruction (gate G2). Each is an OR of basic events.

```{.dot include="docs/regulatory/risk/fault-tree-data-loss.dot" caption="Fault tree of hazard H-01. Five events lose data, three hide the loss; each minimal cut set needs one of each, unless an event belongs to both." width="80%"}
```

The **minimal cut sets** are the smallest sets of basic events that cause the top event. As drawn,
there are fifteen, each pairing one loss (E1 to E5) with one way of missing it (E6 to E8), so no
single event causes the top event. That is the picture the design intended, and it is wrong for
one event. E5, open windows never closed at the end of a run, loses data on a path that has no
counter, so E5 also causes E6: drawn honestly, with E5 under both gates, it is a minimal cut set
of order one, a single point of failure. The integrity verdict cannot detect what was never
counted. The control for E5 has to prevent it instead, and the risk file now has it as RC-20:
SRS-PIPE-012 requires that a stop processes all data already read, its test runs on every
change, and the sorter's tests check its accounting identity (SRS-SRT-009), which fails when a
single is left in an open window. A common cause that the drawing does not show is the usual way
a single point of failure hides in a tree, and Exercise 15.4 has you find it with code.

### Feeding the risk file

Both analyses end in the hazard file. The FMEA row about out-of-order singles is the cause of
H-02's sequence; the fault tree's single point became a control of H-01 of its own, RC-20, which
names SRS-PIPE-012 and its test; the tie rule became a requirement, so that the next change to
the sorter cannot alter coincidences unnoticed (H-07). The risk management plan lists the FMEA
and the fault tree as activities with their outputs, so an auditor can see which technique produced which
input, and so that the next change to the sorter knows where to update.

```

Every hazard has every field and sits on the scales; a closed hazard has an acceptable residual

inspected document exists; every hazard refines a preliminary hazard of the classification, and

The trace matrix gains a column. Each requirement now lists the risk controls it implements, so
the reviewer of a change to SRS-OUT-006 sees at once that two controls, RC-04 and RC-14, rest on
it. Today the matrix holds 121 requirements, 119 of them verified by at least one of 146 tests;
32 of them implement one or more of the 20 risk controls.

Like Chapter 14's checks, these judge form, not content. The tool cannot tell whether a hazard is
missing, whether S2 is the right severity, or whether a test really shows that a control is
effective. The plan's section on what the checks do not do says so, and assigns each of those
judgments to a review.

## Residual risk and benefit-risk

### The intuition

After the controls, every hazard has a residual risk, and each is evaluated against the criteria.
Then the standard asks a second question: are all the residual risks together acceptable, and do
the device's benefits outweigh them? The overall residual risk is not a sum. It is a judgment,
supported by the analysis, that someone with the authority to make it signs. A risk matrix helps
the judgment by showing where the risks sit, before and after control:

| Severity | P1 | P2 | P3 | P4 | P5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S4 |  | * | * | * | * |
| S3 |  |  | * | * | * |
| S2 | H-03, H-09 | H-01, H-02, H-04, H-05, H-06, H-07 |  | H-08 * | * |
| S1 |  |  |  |  | * |

Cells marked with `*` are not acceptable by the criterion. Before control, seven of the nine
hazards sat in the S2/P4 cell; after control, all but one moved to P2 or P1.

### A worked example: releasing with H-08 open

H-08, control of the acquisition by an unauthorized party, has no effective control yet. Anything
that can reach the control port can stop or reconfigure a scan; the audit trail records who did
it, with the client's address and port (SRS-CTL-032), but records do not prevent. Its residual
risk, S2/P4, is not acceptable, and the risk file says so: the hazard is open, with the planned
control, authentication and authorization of control clients, in Chapter 17 and problem report
MDQ-PR-008.

Can MiniDAQ 0.3 be released? Yes, because of what it is: a development release for use with the
simulator and test benches, not at a scanner with patients. With no patient, H-08 cannot lead to
harm, and MiniDAQ 0.3's release record will say exactly that. A release intended for clinical use could not be
justified the same way: an open hazard with an unacceptable risk and no benefit that requires it
is a reason not to release. ISO 14971's benefit-risk analysis is for the risk that cannot be
reduced further, such as the dose of the scan itself, whose benefit is the diagnosis. It is not a
way to accept a risk that a known design change would remove.

## After the release

Clause 10 closes the loop. The manufacturer collects information about the device in production
and use, from complaints, service reports, the literature and comparable devices, and reviews it
for new hazards and for risks that are higher than estimated. For the software, the channel is the
problem resolution process of IEC 62304's clause 9 (Chapter 14), which already asks for an
evaluation of safety relevance for every problem. The day in the life is an example: a problem
report whose evaluation reaches back into the risk file and changes a probability, and then a
control. Chapter 16 places that loop inside the quality management system, with complaints,
CAPA and post-market surveillance.

Two habits make this loop work in a software team. Every problem report names the hazards it
touches, so that a trend (three reports in a year about loss on the link) is visible in the risk
file and not only in the tracker. And every change runs `docs.risk`, so a change that removes a
requirement a control relies on, or renames the test that shows a control's effectiveness, fails
before review rather than at the next audit.

The code changed less than the documents: one new function, `assess_integrity`, one audit record,

thought about failure; the risk file finds the few that are missing, joins the rest into
something a consumer can act on, and makes their verification visible.

::: box-regulated
What ISO 14971 means for your daily work as a DAQ engineer:

- **Follow a design choice to the patient.** Before you add a drop policy, a timeout or a default,
  write the chain: what can go wrong, who is exposed, what harm can follow.
- **Never lose data silently.** Count every loss where it happens, and make sure something reads
  the counter: a verdict, a fault, an alarm.
- **A risk control is a requirement.** It has an ID, a test that verifies it, and a test or
  analysis that shows it is effective. Tag both.
- **Look for what your control breaks.** A new fault interrupts scans; a strict verdict invites
  being ignored. Record the trade in both hazards.
- **Changes reach the risk file.** If your change touches a requirement that implements a control,
  the reviewer must see it; `docs.risk` and the trace matrix's column make sure they do.
- **Field problems are risk information.** Name the hazards in every problem report, and ask
  whether the estimate still holds.
:::

## Summary {.unnumbered}

- ISO 14971:2019 is one risk management process for the life of a device: plan, analysis,
  evaluation, control, overall residual risk, review before release, and information from
  production and use; ISO/TR 24971:2020 is its guidance.
- A hazard analysis follows a chain from hazard through a sequence of events and a hazardous
  situation to harm; software is almost always a step in the sequence, and defects are causes,
  not hazards.
- Software failure is taken as certain; the probability of harm comes from whether the failure is
  detected and what happens in the clinic, against criteria written before any rating.
- Risk controls are chosen in order: inherent safety by design, protective measures, information
  for safety; most software controls detect a failure rather than prevent it.
- A control is verified twice: its implementation, through the requirement's tests, and its
  effectiveness, through a test or analysis that starts from the hazard.
- Controls can create risks; MiniDAQ's fault on out-of-order singles trades wrong coincidences
  for interrupted scans, and the risk file records the trade in both hazards.
- An FMEA against the code finds causes the top-down analysis misses (the sorter's tie rule); a
  fault tree finds combinations and single points of failure (closing the windows at the end of a
  run).
- The overall residual risk is a signed judgment, not a sum; a benefit-risk argument is for risks
  that cannot be reduced, not for skipping a known design change.
- MiniDAQ now has nine hazards and twenty controls, an integrity verdict in every run record,
  seven new requirements, a sorter FMEA, a fault tree, and a check that keeps all of it in step
  with the code and the trace matrix.
