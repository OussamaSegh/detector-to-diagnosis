## Exercise 10.1: A reference sorter, checked against the truth


**Key points.**

- Apply the energy window first and form windows over the accepted singles only. A
  scattered 300 keV photon between two good singles would otherwise turn a clean pair into a
  multiple, and the pair would be lost.
- After a window closes, the next one opens at the first single after it, whatever the
  window held: `i += n`. The starter's `i += 1` let the third single of a window open a new
  window and pair with a fourth.
- Classify from the truth: different decays make a random; the same decay with a scattered
  photon a scatter; otherwise a true.
- The test asks for exact agreement with the Chapter 8 truth counter and with
  `CoincidenceSorter`. With identical rules, one pair of difference is a bug, and the list of
  differing pairs points straight at the rule.

**Common wrong answers.** An exclusive window (`<` instead of `<=`); filtering by energy
after pairing; a window measured from the previous single instead of the opener, which is a
paralyzable window that grows while singles keep arriving; calling every same-decay pair a
true.

## Exercise 10.2: Multiples policies

```{.cpp include="exercises/ch10/e2_multiples_policies/solution/policies.cpp" snippet="resolve"}
```

**Key points.**

- Build the list of good pairs once; each policy is then a one-line rule over it: none, all,
  exactly one, or the best by summed energy. The four cannot disagree about which pairs are
  good.
- The winner needs a tie rule. "Strictly greater" keeps the earlier pair, which is what
  `CoincidenceSorter` does, so the results match exactly.
- Without a geometry cut every pair is good, so a window of three has three good pairs and
  keep if only one good never keeps anything: it equals kill all.

**The measurement.** With the sector cut and a 120 mm field of view, at 40 MBq: take all
goods 379,260 trues/s and 22.5% randoms in the prompts; winner of goods 368,660 and 20.4%;
kill all 358,920 and 18.6%; only one good 359,120 and 18.6%. At 1 MBq the policies differ by
less than the counting noise. A good paragraph chooses with a reason (for example the winner
of goods: a part of the trues of take all goods with fewer of its randoms), says that the
choice belongs to the physics team together with the randoms model, and notes that the
delayed-window estimate is biased differently for each policy (+5.0% for kill all, −8.1% for
take all goods at 40 MBq), so the correction must be matched to the policy.

**Common wrong answers.** Counting a window's pairs before checking that they are good;
choosing the winner by the time difference rather than the energy (a defensible rule, but not
the one specified); a plot of trues alone, which always favors take all goods.

## Exercise 10.3: A parallel sorter that loses and doubles nothing

```{.cpp include="exercises/ch10/e3_parallel_slices/solution/parallel_sorter.cpp" snippet="parallel"}
```

**Key points.**

- **The cut rule.** If two consecutive accepted singles are more than a window apart, the
  window that holds the first cannot hold the second, so the second opens a new window
  whatever came before. A sorter started there produces exactly the serial windows. Cut at
  the first such gap after each nominal boundary.
- **Measure the gap between accepted singles.** A rejected single in the middle of a pair
  does not separate it; a cut there splits a real pair.
- **Read ahead, then drop.** Each worker sorts up to delay + window beyond its slice, so the
  delayed windows of its last openers are complete, and then removes every coincidence whose
  opener is at or after the next slice's first single. Each coincidence has exactly one
  opener, and each opener lies in exactly one slice: nothing is lost, nothing doubled.
- **Order.** Slices are joined in time order and each slice's output is in opener order, so
  the joined list equals the serial list element by element.
- **Without gaps** (a hot source, a wide window) `slice_starts` returns fewer slices than
  threads and the work stays serial. Per-single windows have no history: any cut works.

**Common wrong answers.** Cutting at equal sizes and checking only total counts, which agree
to within one pair in a hundred thousand on realistic data; read-ahead without dropping, which
doubles the coincidences opened in the overlap; dropping by the second single's time instead
of the opener's.

**Key points.**

- **Profile first.** On the book's VM the baseline spent 53.2% in `close_ready` indexing a
  `std::deque`, 26.5% in `malloc` and `free` for two vectors per window, 12.0% in `sincos`,
  7.0% resolving windows and 0.5% in the `std::function` callback. The last is the one most
  people suspect first.
- **The changes.** A vector buffer with indices and one compaction every few thousand
  singles; windows as index ranges, with no allocation in the steady state; crystal positions
  and sectors from tables, and the field-of-view test on squared distances; the delayed
  window's start index carried forward; coincidences appended directly.
- **The result.** 40.7 to 41.3 M singles/s inside the energy window against 16.0 to 16.7 for
  the baseline, 2.47 to 2.55 times as fast, with the coincidences unchanged.
- **The report** must give the machine and flags, the profile with numbers, each change
  measured on its own, the spread over repetitions and the next step. The solution's
  `NOTES.md` is an example; it measured the changes together and says so, which a report on
  your own work should not do.

**Common wrong answers.** Optimizing the callback first; measuring with every single inside
the energy window, which inflates the rate; dropping the delayed window or a policy to win
speed (the correctness tests catch it); one run instead of the best or median of several.

## Exercise 10.5: Merge by time slices, and measure it against the heap

```{.cpp include="exercises/ch10/e5_slice_merge/solution/slice_merge.cpp" snippet="slices"}
```

**Key points.**

- Half-open slices [T, T + Δ) put every single in exactly one slice; the loop runs until
  every single is out, so the last partial slice is never lost.
- Gather each slice board by board, in board order, and sort it with `std::stable_sort` by
  `TimeOrder`. Equal singles then keep board order, which is the heap merger's tie rule.
- Measured on the book's VM (medians of five): heap 30.4, 13.3 and 10.1 M singles/s for 4,
  24 and 72 boards; slices 48.4, 28.4 and 20.6. About twice as fast for many boards, at the
  cost of up to one slice width of extra latency.

**Common wrong answers.** Closed slices [T, T + Δ], which emit edge singles twice;
`std::sort`, which passes most tests and fails on ties; comparing the two mergers on
different inputs or with different chunking.

**Key points.**

- A single in detector 1 at t pairs with any single in detector 2 in [t − τ, t + τ], an
  interval of 2τ: R = 2τS~1~S~2~, 8,000 per second for 1 M/s in each detector and 4 ns.
- Count partners on both sides of each single; the starter's one-sided count and its τS~1~S~2~
  are wrong by the same factor and agree with each other, which is why the tests include a
  hand-made case.
- With trues added, the singles of the true pairs make randoms with everything else like any
  single, so the formula, which counts all singles, stays right; prompts minus delayed equals
  the trues.
- MiniDAQ's sorter looks only forward from each single, but between two detectors a pair
  with either one first is found from its earlier single: together the windows cover
  |Δt| ≤ τ, the same 2τ.

**Common wrong answers.** Using only background singles in the formula; a delayed offset
shorter than the time spread of the trues; comparing the estimate with the prompts instead of
with the prompts minus the trues.

## Check yourself: model answers

1. **Time order before windowing.** A streaming sorter decides that a window is complete when
   a single arrives that is too late to belong to it. That proof only works if nothing
   earlier can still arrive. Without order, any window could still gain a partner, and the
   sorter would have to hold the whole run.
2. **A board 10 µs late.** With a merger that may wait 100 µs: nothing is lost; the merger
   holds about 10 µs of the other boards' data and the output is 10 µs later. With a bound
   of 5 µs: the watermark runs ahead and almost all of that board's singles are counted as
   late and dropped. Without a merger: the sorter sees them out of order, counts and drops
   them (or, without the check, pairs them with the wrong singles). Every coincidence involving
   that board's crystals is lost, which shows in the sinogram as missing bands and in
   per-board counters at once, but hardly in a global rate.
3. **Fixed and per-single differ.** Window 4 ns, singles at 0, 3, 6 and 20 ns. Fixed: 0
   opens [0, 4] with 3, a pair; 6 alone; 20 alone. Per single: (0, 3) and also (3, 6). Any
   chain of singles each within the window of the previous one, but not of the first, works.
4. **One true at most.** One annihilation makes two photons, so among three singles at most
   two come from one decay; the third pairs randomly with both. Take all goods therefore adds
   at most one true and up to two randoms per multiple: at high activity its extra prompts are
   mostly randoms.
5. **Only one good equals kill all without cuts.** Every pair is good, a window of three has
   three good pairs, never exactly one, so every multiple is discarded.
6. **2τS~1~S~2~.** Detector 2's singles within τ before or after a single of detector 1 make a
   pair: an interval of 2τ, so 2τS~2~ partners per single and 2τS~1~S~2~ per second. The sorter's
   forward-looking window catches the pairs with detector 2 first from detector 2's single.
7. **Kill all reads high.** A prompt window with a true and a random is discarded, so its
   random never reaches the prompts, but the delayed window of the same opener still counts a
   random as usual.
8. **96 positions apart.** 90° apart on a 150 mm ring: the chord is 150 cos 45° = 106.1 mm
   from the axis, inside 120 mm, so it passes. The distance is 150 cos(πΔ/384), at most
   120 mm for Δ ≥ 79 positions.
9. **The watermark.** Every single earlier than the watermark has been received from every
   board, because each board's stream is in order and every board has reported a later time.
   A board that has never reported could still send anything, so there is no watermark until
   all have.
10. **A gap as a cut.** After a gap wider than the window, no window can contain singles from
    both sides, so the history before the gap cannot influence the windows after it. Rejected
    singles take no part in windows, so the gap is measured between accepted singles; a gap
    measured across a rejected single could split a real pair.
11. **Counters that do not add up.** There is a bug, even if the coincidences match: a path
    that drops or double-counts singles without emitting anything different on this data. Find
    it before it matters on other data; the accounting identity is part of the specification.
12. **Software or firmware.** Software: rules changeable in a release, testable against a
    simulator, singles recordable so a run can be sorted again. Firmware: a much smaller data
    rate to the PC, and a fixed, short latency.
