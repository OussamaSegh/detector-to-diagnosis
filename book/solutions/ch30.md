**Key points.**

- Take the maximum from the parabola through the highest sample and its neighbors; on a flat top
  or at either end, take the sample.
- Walk outward from the highest sample to the first sample at or below the level on each side, and
  interpolate between it and the sample before it.
- Refuse a profile that never falls below the level: the width would be the profile's length.
- Return mm: multiply by the step, and report the pixel with the width.

**Common wrong answers.** Counting the samples above the level, which gives whole samples and jumps
by one sample as the peak moves. Using the highest sample as the maximum, which puts the level 5.5%
low for a peak between samples at 3.5 samples per FWHM, and the FWHM about 4% wide. Interpolating
between the crossing sample and the peak, instead of its neighbor.

## Exercise 30.2: Contrast and noise from regions of interest

```{.python include="exercises/ch30/e2_regions/solution/regions.py"}
```

**Key points.**

- Subtract one from both the measured and the true ratio: contrast is what is above the
  background.
- Take each region's mean first, then the mean and the sample standard deviation (K - 1) of the
  means.
- Report the variability per region size: a region's mean averages its pixels down.

**Common wrong answers.** The ratio of the ratios, which reads 0.25 for no contrast. The standard
deviation of the pooled pixels, which is the pixel noise and the same for every region size. The
population standard deviation of twelve means, 4% smaller than the sample's.

## Exercise 30.3: Acceptance limits and a trend

```{.python include="exercises/ch30/e3_qc/solution/qc.py"}
```

**Key points.**

- The limits come from the baseline alone, with the sample standard deviation, and need enough
  results to mean something.
- The results under review are compared with the limits, never added to them.
- A run counts results on the same side of the baseline's mean, starts again on a crossing, and is
  reported once, when it reaches its length.

**Common wrong answers.** Limits from all the results, which a drift widens as it goes. A run that
counts results on the same side as the previous one, without the baseline's mean. A run reported at
every result after the seventh, which buries the finding under copies of itself.
