**Key points.**

- The payload CRC covers the records only. It is stored inside the block header, so it cannot
  cover itself. The header CRC is computed last, over the 28 bytes before it, and so it protects
  the payload CRC too.
- Time offsets are taken from the block's first record, not the file's. A block closes when the
  next offset would exceed 2^32^ − 1 ps (4.29 ms). At 300,000 coincidences per second that
  happens after about 1,300 records, long before the 4,096-record limit.
- Refuse an unknown *major* version and read any minor version. A reader that refused minor
  version 7 would make every compatible addition break every installed reader.
- Check the payload CRC before returning a record. With an intact header the record count is
  trustworthy, so a damaged payload costs exactly one block and the next block starts where the
  count says.
- A damaged block header (Then, by hand) stops this reader, because the record count can no
  longer be trusted. MiniDAQ's reader searches for the next `MDQB` with a valid header CRC and
  reports the gap in the sequence numbers.

**Common wrong answers.** One CRC over the whole block, which cannot be checked until the block
is complete and still covers itself; offsets from the start of the run, which wrap after 4.29
ms; comparing the whole version as one number; decoding the records first and checking the CRC
afterwards, so damaged records have already been returned.

**Key points.**

- For i + j + 1 ≥ 384 the line is seen from the other side: k − 384 and d → 384 − d. Folding the

  that every line of a ring has its own bin fails at once.

- The radial positions are −R sin(πr/N), not r·πR/N. At r = 113 the line is at 119.7 mm, not
  138.7 mm.
- The sinusoid of a point at (x~0~, y~0~) has amplitude √(x~0~² + y~0~²), 47.2 mm here, and the
  phase of its angle, −32 degrees.

  simulator records each photon where it interacted, 5.9 mm deep in the 15 mm crystal on
  average (1/μ − 15e^−15μ^/(1 − e^−15μ^) for μ = 0.087 per mm). Binning with an effective
  radius of about 156 mm instead of the front-face 150 mm removes it.

**Common wrong answers.** Taking the view as k mod 192 (the fold is at 384, and the distance
changes sign); a "shift" read from the fit at one radius; blaming the fit, which is exact for
an ideal sinogram.

**Key points.**

- Two threads. The caller only encodes and swaps a full buffer for a spare one. The I/O
  thread writes and decides when to fsync. A pool of 32 buffers of 64 KiB (2 MiB) bounds the
  memory, and when it runs dry the caller waits: backpressure, never loss.
- One fsync must cover many blocks. With a 20 ms fsync per 64 KiB block the starter manages
  about 200,000 coincidences per second; moving the same work to another thread would only
  move the bottleneck.
- Bound the unsynced data in time, not in blocks. A bound in blocks holds at only one rate. The
  I/O thread waits with a deadline (`wait_until`), so a trickle of data is synced on time even
  if no new block arrives.
- A write error makes the sink faulted. The I/O thread stops writing but keeps returning
  buffers, so the caller cannot hang.
- On the book's VM every fsync policy sustains 18.6 to 20.5 M coincidences per second over
  3.2 GB, seven times the sorter's 2.7 M per second at its limit (`measurements/ch12/writer.txt`).
  A sensible choice is `interval` with a time bound in production and `close` for development,
  with the bound and the disk measured on the acquisition PC.

**Common wrong answers.** An fsync per block on another thread; a condition-variable wait with
no deadline, so a trickle is never synced; dropping blocks when the pool is empty; measuring
with a run that fits in the page cache; ignoring the error of the final fsync.

**Key points.**

- zlib's CRC-32 starts the register at 0xFFFFFFFF and inverts it at the end. With a start of
  0, leading zero bytes leave the CRC unchanged. The final inversion is why the CRC of nothing
  is 0, and why continuing a CRC in pieces must undo it first.
- A register that shifts right processes the least significant bit first and needs the
  polynomial with its 32 bits reversed: 0xEDB88320, not 0x04C11DB7. The wrong constant is a
  valid CRC of another polynomial, so it passes the affine and burst tests and matches nobody.
- For messages of one length the CRC is affine: crc(a ⊕ b ⊕ c) = crc(a) ⊕ crc(b) ⊕ crc(c). The
  change an error makes does not depend on the message, so two flips cancel exactly when their
  single-flip changes are equal. Counting equal values among 8,192 replaces 33.5 million CRCs,
  and none are equal in 1 KiB.
- With two terms the constant cancels: crc(a ⊕ b) = crc(a) ⊕ crc(b) ⊕ crc(zeros of that length).
- Forging: the CRC of prefix + w is affine in the 32 bits of w, and the 32 × 32 matrix over
  GF(2) is invertible, so any target has exactly one w; `NOTES.md` gives the code.
  `forge(b"record payload that someone edited", 0xDEADBEEF)` is `73 1a 8c f7`. A CRC detects
  accidents; tamper evidence needs a keyed MAC or a signature, and a key someone must manage.

**Common wrong answers.** Trying all pairs, which takes hours in Python; claiming the CRC
detects "all errors"; using the CRC as an integrity check against an adversary.

**Key points.**

- A block is trusted only when both CRCs are right and its sequence number is the next one. A
  power loss can leave a file's new length without its data. A block that looks complete but
  holds zeros would otherwise decode as coincidences at crystal 0 with energy 0.
- Truncate after the last intact block, then append the closing block. Appending after a torn
  tail leaves garbage in front of the end of the run.
- A hole in the segment numbers, two partial files or a damaged complete file are not crash
  states. The sink renames a segment before creating the next, so a crash leaves at most one
  `.part`, the last. Report these states and change nothing.
- A `.part` that already ends with a closing block crashed between the close and the rename:
  rename it, do not close it twice. A `.part` shorter than its header holds no records.
- Truncation destroys only bytes after the last intact block, which no reader would return.
  That is safe because the sink writes strictly in order at the end of the last `.part`. A
  file damaged in the middle breaks that assumption, and the tool must stop.
- A real crash: in seven kill trials, six `.part` files were closed and renamed with every
  record kept, and one was only renamed. The crash measurement shows that a kill can also leave
  a truncated block (7 of 20 trials in `measurements/ch12/crash.txt`).

**Common wrong answers.** Deleting the `.part` file; "repairing" a damaged complete file by
truncating it; renaming without fsync; recovering each file alone without looking at the run.

## Check yourself: model answers

1. A sinogram has a fixed size and throws away time, gates, event energies and the exact
   crystal pairs. List mode allows framing a dynamic study after the scan, cardiac and
   respiratory gating, motion correction, a new energy window or TOF binning, reapplying a
   corrected calibration, and event-by-event reconstruction.
2. The format and its version; the software and its version; the run's start time in UTC and
   the acquisition clock's relation to it; the scanner's geometry and crystal numbering; the
   whole effective configuration (windows, delay, policies, calibration identifiers); whether
   the data are simulated; and, at the end of the run, the counters of the link and of every
   stage. In the file, because a separate file gets lost or mismatched.
3. A 64-bit absolute time in every record would cost 4 bytes of 16. Offsets from a 64-bit
   base per block give the same precision. At low rates a block closes every 4.29 ms on the
   time limit, so it holds fewer records and the 32-byte block header weighs a little more:
   0.2% for the 20 MBq cylinder.
4. With an intact header the record count is trustworthy, so the reader skips exactly that
   block and counts its records as lost. With a damaged header it cannot know where the next
   block starts, so it searches for the next `MDQB` whose header CRC is right.
5. Minor: anything an old reader may ignore, such as a metadata member, a reserved tag kind or
   an ignorable flag. Major: anything that changes the meaning or layout of existing bytes. The
   version is checked first because a new major version may place the sizes elsewhere.
6. The angle drops by 180 degrees (k − N) and the signed distance changes sign (d → N − d).
   Without the sign flip, half the lines land on their mirror bins, some bins get two lines
   and others none, and a point source draws two broken sinusoids.
7. A line with index r lies at −R sin(πr/N), so the spacing shrinks from 1.23 mm at the center
   to 0.74 mm at the edge of the field of view. Arc correction resamples the sinogram to even
   radial spacing.
8. A table of counts by the rings of the two crystals, 48 × 48. SSRB assigns every line to the
   slice halfway between its rings (a + b), ignoring its obliqueness, which blurs the axial
   position of sources away from the axis.
9. The delay is removed first: 100,250 − 100,000 = 250 ps. τ is then +250 or −250 ps,
   depending on which crystal is endpoint a in the sinogram's convention. The delay is not a
   time of flight: the second single of a delayed pair was taken 100 ns later.
10. The rename makes the file look complete. Without an fsync first, a power loss can leave the
    complete name pointing to missing data. The name is an entry in the directory, which the
    file's fsync does not make durable, so the directory is synced after the rename.
11. The 19 M per second with fsync on a long run: the short run fits in the page cache and
    measures memory, while the long run is larger than the kernel lets stay dirty and waits for
    the disk.
12. After a failed write-back, Linux may mark the pages clean, so a second fsync can succeed
    without the data ever reaching the disk, as the PostgreSQL developers found in 2018. Only a
    fault reports the loss.
13. Anyone who changes the data can recompute the CRC, or adjust four bytes to keep it. Tamper
    evidence needs a keyed MAC or a signature with a protected key.
14. At the link between a run and a patient, held by the scanner's workflow software and
    the DICOM images. MDQ-L1 files are outside it: they hold no patient identity and are named
    by run.
