## Exercise 39.1: Where along the line

```{.python include="exercises/ch39/e1_where_on_the_line/solution/where.py"}
```

**Key points.**

- ℓ = c (t~a~ − t~b~) / 2 from the middle, towards b: the photon that arrived later travelled
  further, so the annihilation is nearer the crystal that saw its photon first. Each photon's path
  changes by ℓ, so their difference changes by 2ℓ: the factor ½.
- Clamping to the segment keeps a point inside the scanner when timing noise near a crystal gives
  a τ longer than the line. A reconstruction does not clamp: its kernel has tails beyond the line,
  and the image simply has no pixels there.
- floor((2τ + K w) / 2w) is the histogrammer's formula, in integers there. Python's `round` rounds
  half to even, and `int()` truncates towards zero; both put a negative τ on an edge in the wrong
  bin.

**By hand.**

1. 250 ps is 0.2998 × 250 / 2 = 37.5 mm along the line; 300 mm / 37.5 mm = 8 resolution elements.
2. With the earlier single as a, τ is never negative and the information moves into which crystal
   is called a; one sinogram bin then holds events of both orientations, and their τ cannot be
   added. Fixing a by geometry gives every bin one orientation, so τ adds up in it.
3. A uniform rounding of 5 ps has a standard deviation of 5 / √12 = 1.4 ps per time stamp, 2 ps on
   a difference, 0.3 mm along the line: nothing against the kernel's 19 mm.

**Common wrong answers.** c τ instead of c τ / 2. Moving towards the crystal whose photon arrived
later. Bins centered on multiples of the width instead of having their edges there.

## Exercise 39.2: The TOF kernel

```{.python include="exercises/ch39/e2_tof_kernel/solution/kernel.py"}
```

**Key points.**

- A time difference τ places the point at c τ / 2, so every conversion from picoseconds to
  millimeters along a line carries the ½: the resolution, the bins' width, the edges.
  σ = 300 / 2.3548 × 0.2998 / 2 = 19.1 mm.
- Integrate, do not sample. The two differ little when the bins are much narrower than the kernel;
  with bins as wide as it, sampling is a few percent off per bin, and with wider bins the sampled
  weights add up to far more than one (2.8 for the middle bin of the test).
- One erf per edge: K + 1 calls for K bins, and the sum telescopes to the difference of the outer
  two, so it is one to rounding for a point well inside. MiniDAQ's C++ projector does the same and
  skips the bins beyond six standard deviations.

**By hand.**

1. Integration gives the exact probability of the bin; sampling approximates it by the density at
   one point. The difference is of the order of (w / σ)² / 24 relative for narrow bins, and matters
   once the bins are a sizable fraction of σ, or wider.
2. Bins of 20 ps cover the same 2.1 ns with 105 bins: five times the memory and about five times
   the projection's time. The gain is the binning's loss of precision, 15 mm of bin against a
   kernel of 45 mm FWHM already, so almost nothing; list mode removes it entirely.
3. Counts in the real kernel's tails are, for the model, impossible so far from their place: the
   reconstruction explains them with activity elsewhere along the line. The image gets faint
   halos and biased values, and less of the expected noise reduction. A kernel a little too wide
   costs only some of the gain; one too narrow invents structure.

**Common wrong answers.** c instead of c / 2. The density at the bin's center times its width.
Normalizing the sampled weights to one, which hides the error for a point at the middle and not
for one near the edge of the bins.

## Exercise 39.3: Block clock offsets from a point source

```{.python include="exercises/ch39/e3_block_offsets/solution/calibrate.py"}
```

**Key points.**

- Take out what the geometry predicts first. For a source at the center the two paths are equal
  and the prediction is 0; 60 mm off center it reaches 2 × 60 mm / c = 400 ps, larger than the
  offsets being measured.
- One least-squares fit of all the offsets, not a mean per block: the leftover of an event is
  o~a~ − o~b~, so a block's mean leftover is its own offset less the mean of its partners'. The
  normal equations have the Laplacian of the block pairs, as the time base monitor's (Chapter 38,
  Exercise 38.3), here with one equal weight per event.
- Differences cannot measure the common constant: fix the mean at zero over the blocks that saw
  events. A block with no event has no equation and is left out of the system, not set to zero,
  which would pull the mean.
- Check that the events connect the blocks. A point source at the center is the obvious choice and
  the wrong one: every line through it joins a block to the one opposite, so the data hold four
  independent pairs, and a solver with a mean of zero would still return eight numbers.

**By hand.**

1. The point source's position makes the expected τ of every event known, so what is left is the
   blocks' offsets themselves, whatever their age. The monitor has no known source: it can only
   compare each pair with its own beginning, so it sees changes and not a constant offset.
2. One event measures o~a~ − o~b~ with the pair's timing noise, σ = 300 / 2.3548 = 127 ps. With many
   partners, a block's offset has σ ≈ 127 / √n, so 5 ps needs n ≈ (127 / 5)² ≈ 650 coincidences
   per block, more since the partners' offsets are uncertain too.
3. A source 2 mm away from where it is assumed changes the predicted τ of a pair by up to
   2 × 2 mm / c ≈ 13 ps, smoothly with the line's direction; the fit absorbs part of it into the
   offsets. 13 ps is 2 mm along a line, nothing against a kernel of 45 mm, but more than the 5 ps
   that question 2 aimed for: know the source's position to a fraction of a millimeter.

**Common wrong answers.** A mean per block. Fixing one block at zero. Answering for a source at the
center. Including events with both singles in one block, which carry no information about it.
