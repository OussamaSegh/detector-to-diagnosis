**Key points.**

- Two equations in two unknowns: `t2 - t1` is the delay plus the offset and `t4 - t3` the delay
  less the offset. Half their difference is the offset, half their sum the delay. The slave's
  turnaround `t3 - t2` cancels.
- The derivation assumes the same delay both ways. Whatever the two directions differ by, half of
  it goes into the offset, and no exchange of messages can see it, because a different offset with
  a different asymmetry gives the same four time stamps. Cables of 100 m and 80 m (about 5 ns/m)
  differ by 100 ns, so the offset is off by 50 ns.
- Queueing only ever adds to a delay, and adds differently in each direction. Keep the exchanges
  with the shortest round trip, which met the least of it, and average their offsets.
- Hardware time stamping removes the software's own delay from `t1` to `t4`; a transparent clock
  removes a switch's queueing by adding its residence time to the message, so every exchange looks
  like a fast one.

**Common wrong answers.** `t2 - t1` alone, which includes the delay. Averaging every exchange,
which keeps the queueing's asymmetry, microseconds off. A median of the offsets, better but still
pulled by the busy exchanges.

**Key points.**

- The integral term learns the clock's frequency error; once it has, the proportional term has
  nothing left to answer and the offset ends at zero. With the proportional term alone, a clock
  20 ppm fast settles where `kp * offset` cancels 20,000 ppb: about 28.6 us.
- An offset above the step threshold is stepped at once; slewing 5 ms at 500 ppm would take ten
  seconds and at a typical limit of 2,000 ppb forty minutes. The step keeps the integral, since an
  offset says nothing about the frequency.
- While the answer is at its limit, the integral stays as it was (conditional integration).
  Without that it grows for the whole time at the limit, and the clock overshoots for as long
  afterwards.
- The closed loop with `kp = 0.7` and `ki = 0.3` has two poles of magnitude about 0.55. It becomes
  unstable for `kp` above `(4 - ki) / 2`, 1.85 with this `ki`: at 1.5 the offset rings and dies
  away, at 2.5 it grows. A smaller `ki` averages the noise over more updates and takes longer to
  learn a change of frequency.

**Common wrong answers.** Integrating while limited, which passes the frequency tests and fails
the overshoot test. Resetting the integral on a step, which throws away the frequency it learned.
Dividing by `dt` twice, which only shows when the interval is not 1 s.


**Key points.**

- The normal equations of the weighted fit have the graph's Laplacian, which is singular: a group
  of connected blocks can move together without changing any pair. Adding `J/size` per group fixes
  each group's mean at zero, and the inverse less `J/size` is the covariance of the offsets.
- Every pair is used, each with the weight its variance gives it; a spanning tree throws away the
  redundancy that both averages the noise and makes chi2 possible.
- Around any loop of pairs, block clocks give shifts that sum to zero, since each offset enters
  once with each sign. A nonzero sum means something that is not a block clock, a swapped cable or
  a pair-specific delay, and chi2 per degree of freedom shows it.
- A block's sigma falls as it has more partners, since each pair is another measurement of it.
  For two blocks and one pair of variance `v`, each offset is half the shift and its sigma
  `sqrt(v) / 2`; with `v = 400` that is 10.
- In radio interferometry the antennas' phase errors play the role of the block clocks: the
  closure phase around a triangle of baselines cancels them and keeps what the source does.

**Common wrong answers.** Fixing the first block at zero instead of the mean, which gives the
first block a sigma of 0 and every other one too much. Solving the singular system with a
pseudo-inverse over all blocks at once, which mixes two groups. Counting the degrees of freedom as
pairs less blocks, which forgets one constant per group.

## Exercise 38.4: Jitter, wander and the Allan deviation

```{.python include="exercises/ch38/e4_allan/solution/allan.py"}
```

**Key points.**

- The Allan variance is half the mean square of the second difference of the time error over tau:
  an offset and a constant frequency vanish from it.
- Use every start (the overlapping estimator), `N - 2m` terms, for a narrower confidence interval
  from the same record.
- The slope of the curve names the noise: -1 white phase, -1/2 white frequency, +1/2 a random walk
  of the frequency, +1 a linear drift of it.
- The Allan deviation characterizes a time base and the best averaging time; an alarm on the offset
  is what sees a clock that is wrong in a constant way.

**Common wrong answers.** A classical standard deviation of the frequency, which grows with the
length of the record for a drifting clock. Averages of non-overlapping blocks without saying so.
Dividing by `tau` instead of `tau^2` in the variance. Expecting the Allan deviation to flag a clock
that runs fast.
