**Key points.**

- Subtract the background before you fit. Patient scatter rises toward the photopeak from below,
  so the moments of everything in the window put the center 0.7% low. A straight line through two
  bands at 3 to 3.5 sigma on either side is enough over the width of one peak.
- Fit the shape, not the moments. The standard deviation of a Gaussian cut at plus or minus two
  sigma is 0.88 sigma, and a window that follows the result shrinks round after round. A parabola
  fitted to the logarithm (Guo's method) has no such bias; the window only chooses the points.
- A bin of four codes starting at 4k has its middle at 4k + 1.5. Half a bin is 0.15% of the
  photopeak, the size of the whole gain budget.
- Guo's weights help at 3,000 counts and hurt at 300, where the weights are themselves noisy
  counts and sigma comes out 14% wide. The center stays unbiased, which is why the procedure
  checks sigma only against a wide band.

**Common wrong answers.** Bands just outside the window, which take the peak's own tails as
background and make sigma 11.5% too narrow. Bin positions at the bin's first code. A fit over a
fixed window in codes, which is a different share of the peak for a crystal with a low gain.

**Key points.**

- A peak is a local maximum of the smoothed flood, not one of the highest pixels: a bright blob
  has several pixels above the top of a dim one. Smooth first so that single noisy pixels do not
  make maxima of their own.
- Refine each peak by the centroid of its 3 by 3 pixels, and remember that pixel k is centered at
  k + 0.5.
- The light sharing compresses the edges and bends the rows, so a regular grid puts several edge
  peaks in one cell. Order survives: the 16 highest peaks are the first row, and so on, each row
  sorted by column, while a row's sag is smaller than the gap to the next row.
- The lookup table gives each pixel its nearest peak. That puts the boundary halfway between two
  peaks whatever their brightness, which favors the dimmer crystal; a watershed along the valleys
  would not.

**Common wrong answers.** Taking the 256 highest pixels. Dividing the flood into a 16 by 16 grid
of equal cells. Checking only that 256 peaks were found, which a merged pair of edge crystals and
a spurious peak elsewhere can pass.

**Key points.**

- The pairs measure only differences, so each connected part of the graph has its own unknown
  constant. Count the parts (label propagation until nothing changes) and refuse more than one. A
  point source at the center gives 768 parts of two crystals.
- A crystal's mean time difference is its delay minus the mean of its partners' delays, about
  55 ps rms off for a fan of 120 partners with 600 ps delays. Conjugate gradients solve all the
  equations L d = r together, with L applied as degrees times d minus the sums over partners,
  computed with `bincount`; L is never built.
- L is singular: adding a constant to every delay changes nothing. Conjugate gradients still
  work, because r always sums to zero and the start is zero, so every step stays orthogonal to the
  constant. Subtract the mean at the end to fix the constant.
- Set randoms aside by the residual after a solution, not by the raw time difference: drop pairs
  beyond four robust sigmas (the median absolute deviation) and solve again until nothing changes.

**Common wrong answers.** A window on the raw time difference, which drops true pairs whose
delays differ by more than the window and keeps the randoms inside it. A dense matrix of 1,536 by
1,536, which works for the exercise and not for 18,432 crystals. Fixing one crystal's delay at
zero instead of the mean, which makes every delay carry that crystal's noise.
