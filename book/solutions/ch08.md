**Key points.**

- An annihilation at distance x from the midpoint of its line of response reaches the
  nearer crystal 2x / c earlier. x is at most the radius, so |Δt| ≤ D / c: 1000.7 ps for
  300 mm. A symmetric window |Δt| ≤ 1 ns, 2 ns wide, keeps every true of a perfect detector.
- The detector's timing resolution widens the measured difference: with a CTR of 300 ps
  (σ = 127 ps for the difference) and a margin of 3σ the window is 2.77 ns wide. A true
  whose real difference sits exactly on the edge of the window is kept half of the time.
- In practice the activity lies inside the patient, not out to the crystals: for a 200 mm
  field of view the largest real difference is 667 ps. The window still has to cover the
  whole field of view, because the sorter does not know where the decay was.
- What could justify a wider window than 2.8 ns: board-to-board clock offsets and drift,
  time walk at low energies, non-Gaussian tails of the timing distribution, coarse time
  bins in some front-ends. What it costs: randoms grow linearly with the width.

**Common wrong answers.** D / (2c), forgetting that the window is symmetric; using the ring
circumference; adding the CTR (a FWHM) instead of a multiple of σ; answering "4 ns because
everybody uses it".

**Key points.**

- 10% FWHM at 511 keV is σ = 21.7 keV. The solution's window, two σ below the peak and three
  above, is 468 to 576 keV for 10% and 446 to 609 keV for 15%; both keep 97.6% of the
  photopeak.
- The lower edge does the work: below the peak are the scattered photons. A 450 keV edge
  rejects, for an exact measurement, every photon that scattered through more than 30.2°.
  The upper edge only rejects pile-up and costs almost nothing, so it is set wide.
- Scatter still passes, for two reasons: small-angle scatter keeps most of the energy, and
  the detector's resolution spreads scattered photons above any edge. The energy window
  reduces scatter; scatter correction in reconstruction removes the rest.
- The common clinical choice of a lower edge around 425 to 450 keV is a compromise between
  sensitivity and scatter, decided with NECR (Chapter 11), not with the photopeak alone.

**Common wrong answers.** A symmetric window (511 ± 2σ); treating 10% as σ instead of FWHM;
a lower edge above 480 keV with 10% resolution, which throws away a large part of the
photopeak for little scatter rejection.

## Exercise 8.4: Sampling Compton scattering

```{.cpp include="exercises/ch08/e4_klein_nishina/solution/compton.cpp" snippet="sample"}
```

**Key points.**

- E′ = E / (1 + (E / m~e~c²)(1 − cos θ)). For 511 keV: E at 0°, E / 2 at 90°, E / 3 at 180°.
- The Klein-Nishina shape per solid angle, P² (P + 1/P − sin² θ) with P = E′ / E, is at most
  2 at every energy, at θ = 0. A uniform cos θ is uniform in solid angle, so proposing a
  uniform cos θ and accepting with probability f / 2 samples it exactly. At 511 keV about 29%
  of proposals are accepted.
- The chi-square test with 400,000 samples in 40 bins separates a correct sampler from an
  isotropic one by orders of magnitude; the threshold of 80 for 39 degrees of freedom
  corresponds to p ≈ 1.2 × 10^−4^, and with fixed seeds the result does not change from run
  to run.
- Isotropic scattering is the dangerous shortcut: it produces too much large-angle,
  low-energy scatter, which the energy window then removes too easily, so the scatter
  fraction comes out too small.

**Common wrong answers.** Sampling θ uniformly instead of cos θ (not uniform in solid
angle); using the Thomson (low-energy) distribution; a rejection bound smaller than the
maximum of the shape, which silently biases the result toward small angles.

**Key points.**

- On the book's machine the fitted exponents are 0.974 for trues, 0.967 for scatters and

- The sensitivity, trues per decay, falls from 0.0205 to 0.0193 between 1 and 8 MBq: a true

  is a count loss that grows with activity, and the reason the trues exponent is a little
  below 1. Chapter 11 models it with dead time and pile-up.
- R / T grows almost linearly (exponent 0.98); extrapolated, randoms equal trues near 19 MBq

**Common wrong answers.** A linear fit through raw values (a slope in counts per second per

## Check yourself: model answers

1. **Randoms versus trues.** A true needs one decay, so its rate is proportional to the
   activity. A random needs two independent decays within the window: its rate is the
   product of two singles rates, each proportional to the activity, R = 2τ S~1~ S~2~, so it grows
   with the square.
2. **Time of flight.** It localizes the decay along the line of response to Δx = c · CTR / 2
   (45 mm for 300 ps), so reconstruction spreads each count over Δx instead of the whole
   object. The signal-to-noise gain is roughly √(D / Δx): about 2 for an 18 cm head and about
   3 for a 40 cm body at 300 ps, because the gain depends on the object size.
3. **A 20-minute FDG scan.** Activity falls by 2^(20/109.8) = 1.135: trues to 88%, randoms to
   78% (1.135²). Every rate-dependent setting and correction (dead time, randoms estimate,
   any adaptive behavior) has to hold over that range in one scan, and the DAQ must record
   accurate start and stop times for decay correction.
4. **Attenuation along a line.** The two photons together cross the whole object along the
   line, so the probability that both escape is e^(−μ L), where L is the total length through
   the object, whatever the position of the decay. It can therefore be measured once per line
   (from a CT or a transmission scan) and corrected exactly.
5. **25° scatter.** 511 / (2 − cos 25°) = 467 keV. A 435 keV threshold keeps it: energy windows
   reject large-angle scatter only.
6. **Compton edge.** 511 − 511/3 = 341 keV, the largest energy a 511 keV photon can deposit
   in one Compton scatter (straight back). It bounds the Compton continuum of photons that
   scatter in the crystal and escape.
7. **Spatial resolution.** Positron range, non-collinearity, crystal size, parallax.
   Non-collinearity grows with the ring diameter (about 0.0022 × D); parallax gets worse in a
   small ring, because more lines of response cross crystals at steep angles.
8. **LYSO.** High density and atomic number (good stopping power), high light yield and a
   short decay time (good timing, low dead time). Lutetium-176 makes the crystal itself
   radioactive: the DAQ records singles without any source, which is useful for calibration
   and must not be mistaken for a fault or filtered as noise without thought.
9. **Photofraction.** About 0.6² = 0.36 of true pairs have both photons in the photopeak. The
   others have at least one Compton deposit below the window and are lost to it; in the
   simulator the narrow window kept 47% of the wide window's trues, because the wide window
   also loses some of them below its 100 keV edge.
10. **The heap.** Singles are produced in decay order, but flight time and time blur move them
    by up to about a nanosecond. A single is released once its time is earlier than the next
    decay's time minus the largest negative blur (6σ, clamped), because no future decay can
    produce anything earlier.
11. **Missing effects.** Dead time and pile-up: at high activity the measured singles and
    trues would bend down and NECR would peak earlier. Lutetium background: a constant
    singles rate that adds randoms at low activity. Positron range would not change count
    rates, only resolution.
