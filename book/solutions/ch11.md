## Exercise 11.2: Dead time and pile-up in a block detector

```{.cpp include="exercises/ch11/e2_dead_time/solution/dead_time.cpp" snippet="apply"}
```

**Key points.**

- Non-paralyzable: only a recorded event starts a dead time. Paralyzable: every event
  restarts it from its own arrival. The two agree to first order, m ≈ n(1 − nτ), so a test at
  low rate cannot tell them apart; the Poisson tests at nτ = 0.5 can.
- The dead time is the half-open interval [t, t + τ): an event at exactly t + τ is recorded.
  Either convention is defensible, but it must be stated and tested.
- For pile-up, keep the index of the event each block is recording, and add the lost event's
  energy to it.
- On 3 ms of 30 MBq data the result is identical to `sim::DeadTime` for both models: with
  the same rules, one single of difference is a bug.

**Common wrong answers.** A lost event that extends a non-paralyzable dead time; one global
dead time instead of one per block; pile-up added to the most recent event instead of the
one the block is recording.

## Exercise 11.4: Correcting a measured rate for dead time

```{.python include="exercises/ch11/e4_dead_time_correction/solution/correction.py" snippet="invert"}
```

**Key points.**

- Non-paralyzable: n = m / (1 − mτ), only for m < 1/τ.
- Paralyzable: no closed form without Lambert W. The function n e^(−nτ) is monotonic on each
  side of n = 1/τ, so bisection on the requested side is safe; above 1/(eτ) there is no
  solution and the answer is NaN.
- The error amplification (m/n)(dn/dm) is 1 + nτ for the non-paralyzable model and
  1/(1 − nτ) for the paralyzable one: 1.5 and 2 at nτ = 0.5.
- With a 0.5% uncertainty in each measured rate, a 1% correction holds up to nτ = 1
  (non-paralyzable) and nτ = 0.5 (paralyzable).

**Common wrong answers.** m/(1 + mτ), which applies the loss a second time instead of
undoing it; the first-order m e^(mτ), which is good only for mτ ≪ 1; a solver that returns
the high branch, or a number, above the maximum.

**Key points.**

- Utilization is load over capacity, both in the stage's own unit; a margin is the share of
  the capacity kept free, so a 30% margin saturates at 70% utilization.
- Find each stage's saturation activity on its own, by bisection (the loads grow with the
  activity), and compare activities. Capacities are in different units and cannot be
  compared.
- With one Gigabit link the link saturates first, at 88.6 MBq. With 10 GbE the sorter does,
  at 407.2 MBq, or 285.0 MBq with a 30% margin. The writer, whose 5 M is the smallest number,
  saturates only at about 774 MBq.
- The disk is the only stage whose load grows faster than linearly, because randoms grow as
  A²; its saturation depends on the list-mode format of Chapter 12.

**Common wrong answers.** Forgetting the preamble and inter-frame gap (15.0 M singles per
second instead of 14.79 M for one Gigabit link); a margin added to the capacity; a budget
without its assumptions written next to each capacity.

## Check yourself: model answers

1. **Why the NECR peaks.** If T = aA, S = bA and R = cA², then NECR = a²A/(a + b + kcA),
   which rises to a²/(kc) and never falls. The curve falls because trues are lost faster than
   the activity adds them: dead time, pile-up and multiples rejection. Where randoms
   dominate, NECR ≈ T²/(kR), and with a live fraction L per single, T ∝ AL² and R ∝ A²L², so
   the NECR falls as L².
2. **Time or energy window.** For this system the time window, and more so as the activity
   rises: 4 ns to 1 ns gains 39% at 100 MBq and 11% at 30 MBq, while the best lower threshold
   (460 keV) gains about 6% at both. Randoms grow with τ and with A², while the scatter
   fraction depends on the object, not the rate.
3. **400,000 recorded with τ = 1 µs.** Non-paralyzable: n = 400,000 / 0.6 = 667,000 per
   second. Paralyzable: mτ = 0.4 is above 1/e = 0.368, so no true rate gives it, and neither
   branch exists. The unit is not paralyzable with that τ, or τ is shorter than assumed.
4. **Per block against per crystal.** At 100 MBq a crystal sees about 900 singles per second
   (nτ ≈ 0.001), a block about 230,000 (nτ ≈ 0.23). A coincidence needs two live singles, so
   with 21% of the singles lost, about (1 − 0.21)² = 63% of the trues survive, and pile-up
   removes more.
5. **Pile-up.** It adds a lost event's energy to the recorded one, pushing trues out of the
   energy window and moving pairs of scattered photons into it. The scatter fraction shows
   it: 0.40 at 100 MBq with pile-up, against 0.33 without.
6. **Error amplification.** m = n e^(−nτ), dm/dn = e^(−nτ)(1 − nτ) and m/n = e^(−nτ), so
   (dn/n)/(dm/m) = (m/n)/(dm/dn) = 1/(1 − nτ). At nτ = 1 the derivative is zero: the
   recorded rate no longer depends on the true one, and any error in it makes the corrected
   rate arbitrary.
7. **k = 2 and k = 1.** The trues are estimated as prompts minus the randoms estimate. With
   Poisson counts the variance is the sum of the counts: T + S + R from the prompts, plus R
   again if the randoms are estimated with a delayed window of the same width, whose counts
   are as noisy. A smooth estimate (from the singles rates, or a variance-reduced delayed
   estimate) adds almost no variance, so k = 1.
8. **0.5 ns for the line source.** For an annihilation at distance r from the center, the
   two paths differ by at most 2r. At 45 mm that is 90 mm, 0.3 ns, plus the 300 ps FWHM
   coincidence time resolution. A head filling the 120 mm radius field of view needs 0.8 ns
   plus a margin of a few times the resolution's standard deviation, about 1 ns.
9. **Scatter fraction at low activity.** It describes the object and the energy window.
   At high activity pile-up changes energies (0.33 becomes 0.40 at 100 MBq in the harness),
   and on a real scanner the randoms subtraction adds its own error to the scatter
   estimate, so the number would describe the rate instead.
10. **65% and 45%.** Neither is a bottleneck at 200 MBq. The replay source's percentage is its
    own work (clock reads, copies) and is not a limit of the pipeline. The pair stage, at
    45%, is the busiest real stage and has about twice the rate in reserve.
11. **Link, sorter or writer.** The link, at about 89 MBq, because 14.8 M singles per second
    is reached first. The writer's 5 M is the smallest number but counts coincidences, about
    2% of the singles, and saturates near 774 MBq. Compare activities, which all stages share.
12. **Real scanner against harness.** The measurement shows the real front end's dead time
    and pile-up, the real electronics and their correlations, and the biases of the
    standard's sinogram-based estimates of scatters and randoms. The harness shows what each
    loss mechanism costs on its own (dead time, pile-up, the multiples policy, the windows),
    because it knows the truth of every coincidence.
